C.5.1 Theme C · Psychological Skills SL & HL

Goal Setting

How setting clear goals directs attention, sustains effort and builds confidence, and how outcome, performance and process goals work together.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Goal setting is a psychological skill that directs attention and effort to a specific task. Goals differ in their reference point: an outcome goal is judged against other performers, a performance goal against a personal, measurable standard, and a process goal against the technique or strategy being executed. Process and performance goals are usually more controllable than outcome goals, which depend on others. Effective goals are often SMART (specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound), arranged in a hierarchy from long-term to short-term, and reviewed and adjusted over time.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Define goal setting as a psychological skill that directs attention to a task.
  • Distinguish between outcome, performance and process goals by their reference point.
  • Apply the SMART principles to write an effective goal.
  • Explain how goal setting enhances motivation, attention and persistence.
  • Explain the goal hierarchy and the value of combining short-term and long-term goals.
  • HL: Explain goal adjustment, do-your-best goals and the goal-setting paradox.

Deep Dive

Goal setting as a psychological skill

Goal setting is a psychological skill because it can be learned, practised and refined, like a physical skill. A well-set goal does three things: it directs attention to the relevant parts of performance, it sustains effort when progress is slow, and it provides a standard against which the athlete can judge feedback and build confidence. Goal setting links closely to motivation, particularly to achievement motivation (see C.3.1), because a clear goal makes the chance of success more visible.

Outcome, performance and process goals

The three goal types differ in what they are measured against:

Outcome, performance and process goals, with the reference point, an example and how controllable each is. Athletes often combine all three.
  • Outcome goals are judged against other performers, for example winning a race. They motivate, but depend on the opposition and on factors outside the athlete's control, so they can increase pressure and anxiety if they are the only focus.
  • Performance goals are judged against the athlete's own previous standard, for example a personal best time. They are more controllable, because improvement depends largely on the athlete's own effort.
  • Process goals focus on the technique or strategy being carried out, for example a relaxed grip or even pacing. They are the most controllable, and they often lead to better performance and outcomes over time.

A useful way to combine them is an outcome goal as the destination (qualifying for the final), a performance goal as the personal standard (running under 2:05), and a process goal as the action plan (maintaining even pacing and relaxed shoulders).

The SMART principles

Effective goals are often written using the SMART principles. A goal should be specific about what is to be achieved; measurable so progress can be checked; achievable, challenging but realistic for the athlete; relevant to the sport and to the bigger goal; and time-bound, with a deadline or review date.

The SMART principles, with a vague goal compared against a SMART version. The SMART version can be checked, reviewed and achieved or adjusted.

The goal hierarchy

Long-term goals (for example making a national squad in three years) are most useful when they are broken down into medium-term goals (for example improving a scoring average this season) and short-term goals (for example a daily training target). Short-term goals give frequent feedback and a sense of progress, which maintains motivation, while the long-term goal gives direction. Goals should also be recorded, for example in a training diary, and reviewed regularly.

The goal-setting cycle

Goal setting works best as a continuous cycle: set goals, plan the actions needed, monitor progress with logs, statistics or video, evaluate progress against the goal and the target date, and then adjust the goal or the plan. Adjustment is part of the process. Goals that are never checked are easily forgotten, and goals that are never adjusted may become unrealistic or stop being useful.

The goal-setting cycle. Monitoring and evaluation feed directly into adjustment, which leads to new or revised goals.

Goal adjustment, do-your-best goals and the paradox HL

Goal adjustment means changing a goal in the light of evidence: raising it if it has been reached easily, lowering it if it is unrealistic, or replacing it if circumstances have changed, for example after an injury. Adjustment should be based on objective evidence, not on a single bad result.

Do-your-best goals set an aim to give full effort, without a specific numerical target. They avoid the pressure of a fixed standard, but they are less precise, so they give less clear direction and feedback than specific goals.

The goal-setting paradox refers to the observation that setting a goal can sometimes cause problems. A very difficult or highly visible goal may increase anxiety and cause the athlete to focus on the result and lose their process. An athlete may also set goals that make success depend on factors they cannot control, leading to frustration. Good goal setting therefore uses challenging but realistic targets, emphasises performance and process goals, and reviews the goal's effects on the athlete's emotions as well as their results.

Exam Tips

🎯
Name the reference pointOutcome goals are compared with others, performance goals with a personal standard, and process goals with technique or strategy. Say which applies. Check the IB command terms.
📝
Write goals in SMART formFor a question about setting a goal, write one that is specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound, for the sport in the question.
🔗
Link goals to motivation and attentionExplain that goals direct attention to relevant performance cues and sustain effort, rather than just stating that goals "help".
🔁
Include adjustmentFor evaluation questions, say that goals should be monitored, evaluated and adjusted, and why this matters.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't say that outcome goals are always bad. They motivate, but they should not be the only focus, because they depend on others and can raise anxiety. Also, don't write a vague goal such as "do better" and call it SMART.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1Define goal setting in the context of sport.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • A psychological skill that directs attention and effort to a specific task, to improve motivation and performance.
2Distinguish between outcome and process goals in sport.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • An outcome goal is judged against other performers and the result (for example winning the match).
  • A process goal focuses on the technique or strategy being executed (for example keeping a relaxed shoulder on serve). Process goals are more controllable.
3Identify which element of SMART is missing from the goal "Improve my fitness this season", and rewrite the goal to include it.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • "Improve my fitness" is not measurable, and it has no time limit or specific target.
  • Rewritten example: "Increase my 2 km rowing ergometer time from 8:10 to 7:50 by the end of the season in March."
4Explain why a coach might combine outcome, performance and process goals for a sprinter.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The outcome goal (for example qualifying for the final) gives a clear destination and direction.
  • The performance goal (a personal best time) gives a measurable standard that the sprinter controls more directly.
  • The process goal (for example driving the arms and maintaining posture) gives daily actions that build towards the other goals.
5(HL) Discuss why a goal that is too difficult or too visible may reduce performance, and how a coach could address this.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • A very difficult or highly visible goal can increase anxiety and cause the athlete to focus on the result rather than the process.
  • The athlete may feel that success depends on factors they cannot control, which can lower confidence and motivation.
  • The coach can set challenging but realistic goals, emphasise performance and process goals, and adjust targets when evidence shows they are unrealistic.
  • Reviewing the effect of the goal on the athlete's emotions, as well as their results, helps ensure that goals support rather than harm performance.
