B.3.2 Theme B · Injury SL & HL

Interventions Related to Injury

How injuries are prevented, treated and rehabilitated: managing force, reducing inflammation, staged recovery, safe return to sport and concussion management.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Injury interventions cover the whole injury cycle. Prevention reduces the risk of injury by managing the forces on the body, through training, technique, equipment and rules. Initial treatment manages pain, swelling and inflammation after an acute injury. Rehabilitation restores strength, range of movement and function, using therapeutic exercise and other modalities, and surgical repair is used for some severe injuries. A safe return to play depends on healing, function and psychological readiness, not just on whether pain has gone. Concussion needs its own staged return.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Explain how interventions reduce the magnitude, direction, rate and frequency of force.
  • Distinguish between prehabilitation, rehabilitation, inflammation management and surgical repair.
  • Explain the purpose of initial treatment, including the role of inflammation in healing.
  • Describe the phases of rehabilitation and the criteria for a safe return to play.
  • Describe a staged return-to-play protocol for concussion.

Key Terms

PrehabilitationProactive training before injury to reduce its risk or severity.
RehabilitationStructured recovery after injury to restore strength, movement and function.
InflammationThe early healing response that clears damaged tissue. It causes swelling, heat and pain.
Therapeutic modalitiesTreatments such as physiotherapy, manual therapy, taping and bracing used in recovery.
Return to playA staged, criteria-based return to full training and competition.
Graduated return (concussion)A step-by-step return in which each stage must be symptom-free before the next is started.

Deep Dive

Lowering injury risk: managing force

Injury prevention is not simply avoiding impact. Injuries happen when the forces on tissues are too large, applied in the wrong direction, applied too quickly, or repeated too often. Interventions work by changing one or more of these four features:

Force–time curves for a stiff and a soft landing with a similar impulse (illustrative). Spreading the force over a longer time lowers the peak. The table lists four ways that interventions manage force.

Prevention therefore combines several approaches:

  • Warm-up and conditioning: raises tissue temperature and prepares muscles and joints. Strength and proprioceptive (balance and joint position) training improves the body's ability to absorb and control forces.
  • Technique coaching: changes direction and rate of loading, for example landing with the knees bent and in line with the toes.
  • Protective equipment: helmets, shin guards, mouthguards and padding reduce the magnitude of forces on the body. Equipment must fit and be maintained.
  • Rule changes and supervision: limit dangerous tackles or contact, and make sure competition and training are controlled.
  • Load management: progressing training gradually and including rest and recovery reduces repeated stress and fatigue, which lowers the chance of both acute and cumulative injury (see B.3.1).

Prehabilitation, rehabilitation, inflammation management and surgery

Surgery is usually followed by rehabilitation and physiotherapy, because the repaired tissue must be loaded gradually to become strong and functional again.

Initial treatment and inflammation

After an acute soft-tissue injury such as a sprain or strain, inflammation starts straight away. Its job is to clear damaged tissue and start repair, so it should not be stopped completely. The early treatment goal is to control pain and swelling while protecting the injured area. RICE (rest, ice, compression and elevation) is a traditional approach. Current guidance tends to favour protecting the area, relative rest and early, pain-guided movement over complete immobilisation, so athletes should follow the advice of a medical professional rather than a fixed routine. Anti-inflammatory medication can reduce pain, but a medical professional should advise on its use, as some medication may affect healing.

The approximate stages of soft-tissue healing after an injury. The timescales vary with the tissue and the severity of the injury.

Athletes must not return just because the pain has gone. Pain relief can mask tissue that is still healing, so return should consider the stage of healing, functional ability and the risk of re-injury.

Therapeutic modalities and rehabilitation

Rehabilitation uses a range of therapeutic modalities under the guidance of physiotherapists and sports medicine teams. These include:

  • Therapeutic exercise: the core of rehabilitation, progressing from gentle movement to strength, balance and sport-specific tasks.
  • Manual therapy: hands-on techniques to improve joint movement and reduce stiffness or pain.
  • Taping and bracing: support the joint and can limit unwanted movement while tissue heals or during return.
  • Cryotherapy and other passive treatments: used for pain and swelling, but with limited evidence for long-term healing. Their role is mainly symptom control.

Rehabilitation is usually organised in phases:

PhaseMain aimsTypical activities
1: Protection and early recoveryControl pain and swelling, protect the tissue, restore basic movementGentle range-of-motion work, isometric contractions, support as needed
2: Strength and controlRebuild strength, endurance and balance (proprioception)Progressive resistance training, balance and stability exercises
3: Sport-specific returnRestore sport skills and tolerate the demands of the activitySport-specific drills, progressive loading, reactive and change-of-direction work

Criteria for a safe return to play

Return to play should be based on criteria, not a date alone. Common criteria include:

  • Pain-free full range of movement at the injured joint.
  • Strength that is close to the uninjured side. Many clinicians use a limb symmetry index of about 90% or more on tests such as a hop or a force measurement, but the test must suit the sport.
  • Functional performance in sport-specific tasks, such as sprinting, jumping, cutting and landing, without pain or compensation.
  • Psychological readiness, including confidence in the injured area, and a plan for continued prevention work (see B.3.1 on psychological influences).
  • Medical clearance from the appropriate professional.

Returning too early increases the risk of re-injury, because the tissue is not yet strong enough for full loads and the athlete may change their movement to protect the injury, which can create new problems elsewhere.

Concussion management

A concussion is a brain injury caused by a blow to the head or body that transmits force to the brain. Symptoms can include headache, dizziness, confusion and sensitivity to light or noise, and they may appear later. Recovery is not linear and varies between athletes. Any athlete with suspected concussion must be removed from play and assessed by a medical professional.

Athletes then follow a graduated return, with each stage started only when there are no symptoms at the current stage, including after activity. Each stage increases the demand, and any return of symptoms means going back one stage.

A six-stage graduated return to play after concussion. Each stage must be symptom-free before moving on, and medical clearance is needed at each step.

Psychological aspects of recovery

Injury can cause frustration, anxiety, low mood and fear of re-injury. These can affect motivation and adherence to rehabilitation, and they can change movement during the return to sport. Good communication between the athlete, coach and medical team, goal-setting, and gradual exposure to sport all help the athlete recover both physically and mentally.

Exam Tips

🎯
Link the intervention to the forceName the intervention, the feature of force it changes (magnitude, direction, rate or frequency), and the effect on the tissue. Check the IB command terms.
🧩
Distinguish the four termsPrehabilitation is before injury; rehabilitation is after; inflammation management is the early response; surgery is a repair. Don't swap them.
📈
Describe return to play with criteriaGive measurable criteria (range, strength symmetry, sport-specific function, psychological readiness, clearance), not just "when the pain stops".
🧠
Use the staged logic for concussionEach stage must be symptom-free before progressing, and symptoms mean stepping back. Explain why recovery is not linear.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't say inflammation is always harmful and must be stopped. It is part of healing. The aim is to manage its symptoms and protect the tissue while it does its job.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1Define prehabilitation.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • Proactive training or intervention before an injury occurs, to reduce its risk or severity.
2State two ways that protective equipment reduces injury risk.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • It reduces the magnitude of the forces transmitted to the body (for example padding absorbs impact).
  • It protects vulnerable areas from direct contact (for example helmets and shin guards), and it can help distribute force.
3Explain how a change in landing technique can reduce the risk of injury, using the terms magnitude, direction or rate of force.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Landing with the knees bent and the body controlled increases the time over which force is absorbed, which reduces the rate of loading and the peak force.
  • Keeping the knees in line with the toes changes the direction of force so that it is less likely to cause knee valgus and strain on the ligaments.
  • The lower peak force and better direction reduce the stress on the joint and the risk of injury.
4Outline the purpose of inflammation in the healing of an acute soft-tissue injury.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Inflammation clears damaged tissue and starts the repair process.
  • The swelling, heat and pain signal the injury and protect the area while healing takes place. (Inflammation should be managed, not stopped completely.)
5Discuss why an athlete should not return to play as soon as the pain has gone after an injury.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Pain relief can mask tissue that is still healing, so the tissue may not be strong enough for full loads.
  • Return should be based on criteria, including pain-free range of movement, strength close to the uninjured side and sport-specific function.
  • Returning too early increases the risk of re-injury, and athletes may compensate with altered movement that causes new problems.
  • Psychological readiness and medical clearance should also be considered before a full return.
