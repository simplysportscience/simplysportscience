Training Design

Turning physiology into a plan. The principles that separate training that works from training that just tires you out — and how to build a programme that actually adapts the body.

Key Terms

SpecificityTraining must match the demands of the activity — the muscles, energy systems and movements used.
Progressive OverloadGradually increasing the training load so the body continues to adapt.
ReversibilityTraining adaptations are lost if training stops or is reduced ("use it or lose it").
FITTFrequency, Intensity, Time and Type — the variables adjusted to apply overload.
PeriodisationDividing a training year into phases to peak at the right time and manage fatigue.

Principles of Training

Effective training applies a set of core principles:

  • Specificity — training should replicate the energy systems, muscle groups and movements of the sport
  • Progressive overload — the training load must increase gradually to keep driving adaptation
  • Reversibility — gains are lost when training stops, so consistency matters
  • Individualisation — programmes should be tailored to the individual's needs, fitness and goals
  • Recovery — adaptation happens during rest, so adequate recovery is essential

The FITT Principle

Overload is applied by manipulating FITT: Frequency (how often), Intensity (how hard), Time (how long), and Type (which activities). Adjusting these variables progressively ensures the body is continually challenged without being overloaded too quickly.

Supercompensation and Recovery

Training temporarily fatigues the body; during recovery it rebuilds to a slightly higher level — supercompensation. Timing the next session at the peak of supercompensation drives improvement, whereas training too soon risks overtraining and training too late lets the adaptation fade.

Exam tip: When designing a programme in an exam, always justify choices against named principles — e.g. "interval sessions apply specificity for a games player because they replicate the stop-start demands of the sport."

Periodisation

Periodisation divides the training year into phases — typically preparation, competition and transition — each with different volume and intensity. It ensures an athlete peaks for key competitions (often using a taper), manages fatigue, and reduces the risk of injury and overtraining.

Practice Check
1. State what each letter of the FITT principle stands for. +
Model answer points:
  • Frequency — how often you train.
  • Intensity — how hard you train.
  • Time — how long each session lasts.
  • Type — the kind of training performed.
2. Explain the principle of progressive overload. +
Model answer points:
  • The training load must be increased gradually over time.
  • This continually challenges the body beyond its current capacity.
  • This drives ongoing adaptation and improvement; without it, progress plateaus.
3. Explain the principle of reversibility and one way to avoid it. +
Model answer points:
  • Reversibility means training adaptations are lost when training stops or is reduced.
  • It can be avoided by training consistently and maintaining a level of activity during off-seasons or injury (e.g. cross-training).
4. Explain the purpose of periodisation in a training programme. +
Model answer points:
  • Periodisation divides the year into phases with varying volume and intensity.
  • It ensures the athlete peaks for key competitions and manages fatigue.
  • It reduces the risk of overtraining and injury.
