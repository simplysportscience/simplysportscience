Whole Practice

Practising a skill in its entirety — the method that preserves the flow and feel of skills best learned as one piece.

📖What it means

Whole practice is a method in which a skill is practised in its entirety, as a single unit, rather than being broken into parts. The performer rehearses the complete action from start to finish.

It is best suited to skills that are simple, rapid, or 'highly organised' — whose parts are closely interlinked and difficult to separate without disrupting the skill's timing and flow. Practising such skills whole preserves their fluency, timing and feel.

It contrasts with part practice (breaking a skill into components) and combinations such as whole–part–whole. Whole practice suits many discrete skills and more experienced learners, whereas complex or dangerous skills may be better broken down.

🏅Sporting examples

Golf swing

A golf swing is best learned by whole practice — its parts are so interlinked that breaking it up would disrupt the timing and flow.

Cycling

A continuous, highly-organised skill such as cycling is practised as a whole rather than in separate parts.

Contrast

A complex swimming stroke, by contrast, might be broken into parts (part practice) before being combined.

✍️Using the term in context

Match whole practice to highly-organised skills and contrast with part practice:

"Whole practice rehearses a skill in its entirety, suiting simple, rapid or highly-organised skills whose parts are hard to separate, as it preserves timing and flow; complex or dangerous skills may instead suit part practice."

Link the choice to the skill's complexity and organisation.
