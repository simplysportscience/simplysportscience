The Respiratory System

How air gets into the lungs, how oxygen crosses into the blood, and how breathing transforms the instant you start to exercise.

Key Terms

Pulmonary VentilationThe volume of air breathed in or out per minute. VE = tidal volume × breathing frequency.
Tidal VolumeThe volume of air breathed in or out in a single normal breath (~500 ml at rest).
Gas ExchangeThe diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide between the alveoli and the blood.
DiffusionThe net movement of a gas from an area of high partial pressure to an area of low partial pressure.
AlveoliTiny air sacs in the lungs, surrounded by capillaries, where gas exchange takes place.

Mechanics of Breathing

Breathing is driven by pressure changes in the thoracic cavity. During inspiration, the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles contract, increasing the volume of the thoracic cavity and lowering the pressure inside, so air flows in. During expiration at rest, these muscles relax and the process is passive.

During exercise, expiration becomes active: the internal intercostals and abdominal muscles contract to force air out faster, and additional muscles (such as the sternocleidomastoid) assist forced inspiration.

Gas Exchange at the Alveoli

Gas Exchange at the Alveolus

Gas exchange occurs by diffusion across the alveolar–capillary membrane. Oxygen diffuses from the alveoli (high partial pressure) into the blood (low partial pressure); carbon dioxide diffuses the opposite way.

The alveoli are superbly adapted for this: a huge surface area, walls only one cell thick, and a dense capillary network create short diffusion distances and steep partial-pressure gradients.

Lung Volumes

VolumeMeaning
Tidal volumeAir in one normal breath (~500 ml)
Inspiratory reserve volumeExtra air that can be forcibly inhaled
Expiratory reserve volumeExtra air that can be forcibly exhaled
Residual volumeAir that always remains in the lungs
Vital capacityMaximum air exhaled after maximum inhalation

Respiratory Responses to Exercise

Pulmonary ventilation increases sharply with exercise. VE = tidal volume × breathing frequency, and both components rise. At lower intensities the increase comes mainly from a greater tidal volume (deeper breathing); at higher intensities, breathing frequency contributes more.

Exam tip: A common exam error is confusing pulmonary ventilation with gas exchange. Ventilation is the movement of air in and out; gas exchange is the diffusion of gases at the alveoli. Keep them distinct.

Practice Check
1. Describe the process of gas exchange at the alveoli. +
Model answer points:
  • Gas exchange occurs by diffusion across the alveolar–capillary membrane.
  • Oxygen diffuses from the alveoli (high partial pressure) into the blood (low partial pressure).
  • Carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveoli to be exhaled.
2. Explain two structural features of the alveoli that make them efficient for gas exchange. +
Model answer points:
  • Large surface area — provides more space for diffusion to occur.
  • Walls one cell thick — creates a short diffusion distance.
  • (Also acceptable: dense capillary network maintaining steep gradients.)
3. State the equation for pulmonary ventilation and explain how it changes during exercise. +
Model answer points:
  • Pulmonary ventilation = tidal volume × breathing frequency.
  • Both tidal volume and breathing frequency increase during exercise, raising ventilation to meet oxygen demand and remove carbon dioxide.
4. Distinguish between inspiration at rest and during exercise. +
Model answer points:
  • At rest, inspiration is driven by the diaphragm and external intercostals; expiration is passive.
  • During exercise, additional muscles assist inspiration and expiration becomes active, using the internal intercostals and abdominals to move air faster.
