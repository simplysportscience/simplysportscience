Unit 17 60 GLH Internally assessed

Sports Tourism

Students will explore the evolving landscape of sports tourism, including its economic, social, environmental and technological impact on the modern world, ensuring it is sustainable. They will investigate innovative opportunities for enterprise and develop a plan for a modern sports tourism venture.

1

Overview

Sports tourism brings people to places for sport, and sport to places for tourism. It can bring jobs, income and pride to a region, and it can also bring crowds, pressure on natural resources and environmental damage. This unit explores the types of sports tourism and their impacts, how innovation and sustainability are changing the sector, and how to plan a sports tourism enterprise.

The unit is internally assessed. Students need to describe and evaluate the impacts of sports tourism, explain how innovation and sustainability add value, and produce an enterprise plan that is justified by market evidence and presented professionally.

Learning aims explained

Learning aim A: Explore the characteristics and impacts of sports tourism in the modern world

Students learn the three broad types of sports tourism: spectator-based, participation-based and virtual. They study the businesses that serve sports tourists, from transport and accommodation to coaching and equipment hire. They also examine the economic, social and environmental impacts, both positive and negative, at local, national and international levels.

Strong responses Balances the positive and negative impacts of a named event or destination, using evidence.

Learning aim B: Examine the role of innovation and sustainability in sports tourism

Students study technology-enhanced experiences, such as augmented and virtual reality tours, and sustainable practices, such as eco-certification and carbon offsetting. They consider how these add value, appeal and long-term viability to a sports tourism enterprise.

Strong responses Explains how an innovation or sustainability measure adds value for the customer and the business, not just how it works.

Learning aim C: Investigate opportunities and requirements for a sports tourism enterprise

Students identify customer demand and the resources an enterprise needs. They interpret market data to test whether an enterprise is feasible, and justify their conclusions.

Strong responses Uses market data to support a feasibility judgement, rather than assuming demand exists.

Learning aim D: Develop and present a plan for a sports tourism enterprise

Students produce an enterprise plan with clear links to market research and audience needs, then present it in a professional way, using visual communication and supporting documents.

Strong responses Presents the plan clearly, with each section linked to the market evidence.

Worked examples

Sample responses show the standard expected at each level. They are examples, not model answers to copy.

PassA.P2

Explain the economic, social and environmental impacts of sports tourism.

A major international football tournament can boost local income, because visitors spend on hotels, food and transport. It creates jobs in hospitality and event management. Socially, it can build community pride, but it can also bring noise and overcrowding in residential areas. Environmentally, travel and energy use produce carbon emissions, though a venue built to green standards can reduce the long-term impact.
MeritB.M2

Explain how innovation and sustainability enhance the value, and appeal of, sports tourism enterprises.

A surf school that offers an augmented reality video of each lesson gives customers a clear record of their progress, which increases the value of the course and encourages repeat bookings. Offering eco-certified accommodation and a carbon offset for travel appeals to customers who want a responsible holiday. Both measures help the business stand out, and both can be promoted to a growing group of customers who value sustainability.
DistinctionBC.D2

Justify the feasibility of the proposed enterprise, and recommend innovative and sustainable strategies that improve success and long-term viability.

A guided cycling tour of a regional coastline is feasible because local hotels report high demand for activity-based packages, and the regional tourist board predicts growth in visitors who want active holidays. The enterprise can start small with two guides and a fleet of hired bikes, so the financial risk is limited. To improve viability, the tour should use electric bikes to widen participation, and a booking app that offers carbon-neutral options. These strategies respond directly to the market evidence.

Teaching and assessment tips

Tips
  • Name the type of sports tourism before you describe its impacts. Spectator, participation and virtual tourism have different effects.
  • Use both positive and negative impacts. A one-sided account will not reach the higher criteria.
  • For feasibility, show the market evidence first, then the conclusion. A judgement without evidence is weak.
  • Use real local data where possible, and label any assumptions in the plan.
Common mistakes
  • Describing impacts without linking them to a specific type of sports tourism or place.
  • Assuming that demand exists without using market data.
  • Presenting an enterprise plan with no link between the customer needs and the services offered.
8

Assessment approach

This unit is internally assessed. Centres either set their own assignments or use an Authorised Assignment Brief approved by Pearson. Each assignment must include at least one Distinction criterion. Where a student has not reached the expected standard, the Lead Internal Verifier may authorise one resubmission.

Full information on assessment, resubmissions and retakes is in the BTEC Teacher Hub.

Key terms

Multiplier effectMoney spent by tourists being spent again in other businesses, increasing its total impact on the local economy.
Eco-certificationFormal recognition that a business meets environmental standards.
Augmented reality (AR)Technology that adds digital information to a view of the real world.
FeasibilityWhether an enterprise can realistically succeed, given its market, resources and finances.
Carbon offsettingPaying for an action, such as tree planting, that reduces emissions elsewhere to balance those produced.
