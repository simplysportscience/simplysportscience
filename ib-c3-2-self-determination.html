C.3.2 Theme C · Motivation SL & HL

Self-determination

How autonomy, competence and relatedness drive motivation, how motivation ranges from amotivation to intrinsic, and how coaches can support self-determined athletes.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Self-determination theory (SDT) explains motivation by proposing that people naturally seek to satisfy three basic psychological needs: autonomy (feeling in control of one's actions), competence (feeling capable and effective) and relatedness (feeling connected to others). Motivation is not simply present or absent. It sits on a continuum, from amotivation, through four types of extrinsic motivation that differ in how internal they are, to intrinsic motivation, where the activity is done for its own enjoyment. Coaches who meet these needs tend to build more self-determined and persistent athletes.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Define self-determination theory and describe the three basic psychological needs.
  • Place examples of motivation on the continuum from amotivation to intrinsic motivation.
  • Explain why intrinsic motivation supports enjoyment, self-regulation and persistence.
  • Apply the needs to coaching strategies that support autonomy, competence and relatedness.
  • HL: Describe the six mini-theories and explain the effect of rewards on intrinsic motivation.

Key Terms

AutonomyFeeling in control of one's own actions and choices, acting in line with one's values.
CompetenceFeeling capable, effective and able to master challenges.
RelatednessFeeling connected to, and cared for by, others.
AmotivationA lack of intention to act, with no sense that the action is worthwhile.
Extrinsic motivationActing to gain an outcome that is separate from the activity, such as a reward or avoiding punishment.
Intrinsic motivationActing for the enjoyment and interest of the activity itself.

Deep Dive

The three basic psychological needs

SDT proposes that all people need to satisfy three basic psychological needs for optimal functioning and well-being. Satisfying them supports autonomous motivation; frustrating them tends to produce controlled motivation or amotivation.

The three basic psychological needs, what each means, and an example coaching approach for each. Meeting all three supports self-determined motivation.
  • Autonomy is supported by choice and meaningful input: letting athletes select drills, set some of their own goals, or explain why a task matters. Controlling language, such as "you must", tends to undermine it.
  • Competence is supported by achievable challenges and constructive feedback that highlights progress. A task that is far too difficult, or praise that is not connected to real improvement, does not support it.
  • Relatedness is supported by a positive team climate, trust, cooperation and genuine care from coaches and teammates.

The needs work together. An athlete who is given choice but never succeeds may lose competence; one who succeeds in a cold, controlling environment may lose relatedness. Good coaching considers all three.

The motivation continuum

SDT describes motivation as a continuum, which runs from less to more self-determined. Amotivation is the absence of intention. Extrinsic motivation covers four types, from least to most internalised: external (for a reward or to avoid punishment), introjected (to avoid guilt or protect self-worth), identified (because the activity is personally valued) and integrated (because it fits with the person's own values and identity). Intrinsic motivation is the most self-determined form, where the activity is enjoyed for its own sake.

The motivation continuum, from amotivation to intrinsic motivation, with the four types of extrinsic motivation between them. Examples are illustrative.

Intrinsic and more internalised forms of motivation are associated with enjoyment, effort, persistence and self-regulation over time. A young player who is intrinsically motivated is more likely to keep practising when no one is watching, which supports long-term participation.

Applying SDT to coaching

SDT can guide the design of training environments. A coach can move athletes towards more self-determined motivation by offering choice and explanation, providing achievable challenges with informative feedback, and building a supportive team culture. Coaches who rely mainly on external rewards and pressure may get short-term effort, but this is more likely to be controlled, and it can weaken when the pressure is removed.

This does not mean that extrinsic rewards are always harmful. The effect depends on how the reward is perceived, which is part of the HL discussion below.

The six mini-theories HL

SDT is often described as a meta-theory: a set of six related mini-theories that each explain part of motivation. Together they form a broader framework.

The six mini-theories of self-determination theory, with a brief description and an application to sport. Each explains a different aspect of motivation.

Two of the mini-theories are especially useful for exam answers. Cognitive evaluation theory explains how rewards affect intrinsic motivation. A reward that feels informational, such as praise that recognises skill or improvement, can support the athlete's sense of competence and autonomy, and so support intrinsic motivation. A reward that feels controlling, such as a bonus that depends on winning, can make the athlete feel pressured and reduce their sense of autonomy, which can undermine intrinsic motivation. Goal contents theory distinguishes intrinsic goals (growth, relationships and community) from extrinsic goals (image, wealth and status). Intrinsic goals are linked to greater well-being.

Exam Tips

🎯
Name all three needs when askedAutonomy, competence and relatedness. Then link each to a specific coaching behaviour in the sport in the question. Check the IB command terms.
📍
Place examples on the continuumFor questions about types of motivation, say where the example sits (for example identified or introjected) and why. Don't just say "extrinsic" or "intrinsic".
🏆
Evaluate rewards carefullyFor HL questions, say that informational rewards can support intrinsic motivation, while controlling rewards can undermine it. Give a sporting example of each.
🔗
Link needs to long-term outcomesExplain that meeting the needs supports self-determined motivation, which leads to enjoyment, persistence and long-term participation.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't say that extrinsic motivation is always bad. Some extrinsic motivation can be internalised and become self-determined. Also, don't describe the needs as only "fun" or "choice": competence and relatedness are equally important.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1Identify two basic psychological needs in self-determination theory.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • Any two of: autonomy, competence and relatedness.
2Define amotivation.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • A lack of intention to act, with no sense that the activity is worthwhile or that the outcomes are controlled by the person.
3Explain how a coach could support the need for autonomy in a training session.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The coach can offer choices, such as the athlete selecting from a set of drills or choosing a goal area for the session.
  • The coach can explain why the task is useful and use less controlling language, which lets the athlete feel their actions are their own.
  • This supports autonomous motivation, so the athlete is more likely to persist and engage.
4Place an athlete who trains only to avoid criticism from the coach on the motivation continuum, and explain your answer.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • This is external (or introjected) extrinsic motivation, because the athlete acts to avoid a negative consequence rather than for the activity itself.
  • It sits towards the less self-determined end of the continuum, where the reason for acting is controlled by others.
5(HL) Discuss how extrinsic rewards can affect an athlete's intrinsic motivation, using cognitive evaluation theory.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Informational rewards, such as praise for skill improvement, can support the athlete's sense of competence and autonomy, which supports intrinsic motivation.
  • Controlling rewards, such as a bonus that depends on winning, can make the athlete feel pressured and reduce their sense of autonomy, which can undermine intrinsic motivation.
  • The effect therefore depends on how the reward is perceived by the athlete, not only on the reward itself.
  • Coaches should use rewards that recognise progress and effort, so that they support rather than replace intrinsic motivation.
