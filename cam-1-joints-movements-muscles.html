Joints, Movements and Muscles

How the skeleton, joints and muscles produce movement, and how to analyse a movement in full.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Every movement in sport happens at a joint, where two or more bones meet. Most of the joints you need for Cambridge AS Level are synovial joints, which have a fluid-filled capsule that lets the bones move freely. The type of joint determines which movements are possible and how far they can go.

Movement is produced by skeletal muscles that pull on bones across joints. Muscles can only pull, so they work in pairs or groups: one muscle produces the movement, another opposes or controls it, and others stabilise the bones around the joint. Learning to name these roles precisely is central to this topic.

Key Terms

Synovial jointA freely movable joint with a fluid-filled capsule, such as the shoulder, elbow or knee.
AgonistThe muscle that produces a movement (the prime mover).
AntagonistThe muscle that opposes an agonist, relaxing to allow a movement or controlling it.
FixatorA muscle that stabilises the bone at the origin of an agonist, so that the agonist acts efficiently.
Concentric contractionThe muscle shortens while it produces force.
Eccentric contractionThe muscle lengthens while it produces force, usually to control a movement.
Isometric contractionThe muscle produces force without changing length.

1.1 Joints and movements

The synovial joints you need to know, and the bones that articulate at each, are shown below. The shoulder is a ball-and-socket joint; the elbow and knee are hinge joints; the wrist is condyloid; the radioulnar joint is a pivot; the hip is ball-and-socket; and the ankle is a hinge.

Stability and range of movement

A ball-and-socket joint (shoulder, hip) is multi-axial, so it allows movement in many planes and has the greatest range of movement. The shoulder has the greatest range of any joint in the body, but is the least stable, because its shallow socket and looser ligaments allow the head of the humerus to move a long way. The hip has a deeper socket and strong ligaments, so it sacrifices some range for stability.

A hinge joint (elbow, knee, ankle) is uniaxial: it moves in one plane, flexing and extending. Its shape and strong ligaments make it very stable in that plane, but it cannot rotate. A condyloid joint (wrist) allows movement in two planes, and a pivot joint (radioulnar) allows rotation only.

1.2 Functions of muscles

Agonists produce the movement. During a bicep curl, the biceps brachii is the agonist for elbow flexion.

Antagonists oppose the agonist. The triceps brachii is the antagonist during the bicep curl: it must relax to let the elbow bend, and it controls the movement when the arm is lowered.

Fixators stabilise the bone at the origin of the agonist. During a bicep curl, muscles around the shoulder fix the upper arm so that the biceps can pull the forearm efficiently, rather than moving the shoulder.

1.3 Types of muscle contraction

Concentric contraction is when the muscle shortens while it produces force, for example the biceps lifting a dumbbell upwards.

Eccentric contraction is when the muscle lengthens while it produces force. During eccentric contraction the agonist is controlling a movement that is different from the one named for it in section 1.1. When the arm is lowered slowly in a bicep curl, the biceps is working eccentrically to control elbow extension, even though it is a flexor. Concentric and eccentric are the two types of isotonic contraction, because the muscle changes length while producing force.

Isometric contraction produces force without any change in length, for example holding a wall sit or a plank.

1.4 Muscle fibre types

Slow oxidative (SO, type I) fibres contract slowly, produce low force and are highly resistant to fatigue. They have many mitochondria and rich blood supply, and rely on aerobic respiration. They suit long, low-intensity activity such as marathon running.

Fast oxidative glycolytic (FOG, type IIa) fibres contract quickly and produce more force than SO fibres. They can use both aerobic and anaerobic pathways, and are fairly resistant to fatigue. They suit activities that combine endurance with bursts of effort, such as middle-distance running or a rugby forward sustaining high-intensity work.

Fast glycolytic (FG, type IIx) fibres contract very quickly and produce the greatest force, but fatigue rapidly. They rely mainly on anaerobic glycolysis. They suit short, explosive activity such as a 100 m sprint or a weightlifting clean.

Use examples whenever you explain fibre types, and link structure to function: many mitochondria and myoglobin support aerobic work; fast contraction and rapid fatigue reflect the anaerobic fibre’s structure and fuel supply.

1.5 Movement analysis of actions

The specification asks you to analyse an action in a fixed order. Use this sequence every time:

  1. Identify the joint.
  2. State the type of joint and the articulating bones.
  3. State the type of movement.
  4. Identify the working (agonist) muscle, and the antagonist and fixators where relevant.
  5. State the type of muscle contraction.

Worked example, bicep curl (lifting phase): the joint is the elbow; it is a hinge joint formed by the humerus, radius and ulna; the movement is flexion; the agonist is the biceps brachii; the antagonist is the triceps brachii; fixators stabilise the upper arm; and the contraction is concentric.

Exam Tips

🎯
Exam tipName muscles from the specification list and use the agonist for each movement exactly as given in section 1.1.3. Examiners mark the named muscle.
🧠
Exam tipKeep movement and contraction separate. "Eccentric" describes how the muscle is working, not which way the limb moves.
📐
Exam tipWhen asked to analyse, follow the five-step sequence in 1.5 and label each step. This is much easier to mark than a paragraph.
🔁
Exam tipCompare joints using both stability and range. Ball-and-socket joints give range; hinge joints give stability in one plane.

2Explain why the shoulder has a greater range of movement than the elbow.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The shoulder is a ball-and-socket joint, which is multi-axial and allows movement in many planes.
  • The elbow is a hinge joint, which is uniaxial and moves in one plane only.
  • Therefore the shoulder can move through a much greater range, at the cost of stability.

Model answer
  • It is controlling elbow extension, a movement in the opposite direction to its usual action of elbow flexion.
  • The muscle lengthens while producing force, so the arm is lowered in a controlled way.

Model answer
  • Fast contraction, high force production and rapid fatigue, because they are adapted for anaerobic glycolysis.
