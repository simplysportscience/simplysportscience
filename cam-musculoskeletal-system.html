Joints, Movements and Muscles

The exact joints, movements and muscles the exam tests you on — laid out as the tables you'll actually need to recall.

Key Terms

Synovial JointA freely-movable joint — the type examinable at all seven named joints in this topic.
AgonistThe prime mover muscle that contracts concentrically to produce a movement.
AntagonistThe muscle that relaxes to allow the agonist to produce movement.
FixatorA muscle that stabilises a joint or body part so another muscle can act effectively.
Concentric ContractionA muscle contraction where the muscle shortens while producing tension.

You should also be able to compare and explain the stability and range of movement of these joints — ball and socket joints (shoulder, hip) allow the greatest range of movement but sacrifice stability; hinge joints (elbow, knee, ankle) are more stable but limited to one plane of movement.

1.2 Functions of Muscles

Sliding Filament Theory

Three functional roles, each explainable with an example:

  • Agonists — the prime mover, contracting to produce the movement (e.g. biceps brachii during elbow flexion).
  • Antagonists — relax to allow the agonist to produce movement (e.g. triceps brachii during elbow flexion).
  • Fixators — stabilise a joint or bone so agonists and antagonists can work effectively (e.g. core muscles fixating the trunk during a bicep curl).

1.3 Types of Muscle Contraction

Three types, each explainable with an example:

  • Concentric — the muscle shortens while producing tension (e.g. biceps brachii shortening during the upward phase of a bicep curl).
  • Eccentric — the muscle lengthens while producing tension, controlling the movement (e.g. biceps brachii lengthening during the lowering phase of a bicep curl).
  • Isometric — the muscle produces tension without changing length (e.g. holding a rugby scrum position).

Concentric and eccentric are both types of isotonic contraction — you need to be able to differentiate between them. During an eccentric contraction, the agonist is controlling a different movement at the joint to the one it produces concentrically (e.g. biceps brachii eccentrically controls elbow extension, even though its concentric action is flexion).

1.5 Movement Analysis of Actions

You must be able to fully analyse an action at any of the seven named joints, bringing together everything above: identification of the joint, type of joint, articulating bones, type of movement, working (agonist) muscle, the antagonist and any fixators involved, and the type of muscle contraction taking place.

Exam tip: "Analyse" questions want the complete chain — joint → type → bones → movement → agonist → antagonist → contraction type — for a specific phase of a named skill, not just one or two of these elements.

Practice Check
1. Identify the joint type and articulating bones at the hip and knee. +
Model answer points:
  • Hip — ball and socket joint, femur and pelvis.
  • Knee — hinge joint, femur and tibia.
2. Describe the movement and main agonist at the elbow during the upward phase of a bicep curl. +
Model answer points:
  • The movement is flexion.
  • The main agonist is biceps brachii.
3. Explain the roles of the agonist, antagonist and fixator during a bicep curl. +
Model answer points:
  • The biceps brachii acts as the agonist, contracting concentrically to flex the elbow.
  • The triceps brachii acts as the antagonist, relaxing (lengthening eccentrically on the way down) to allow the movement.
  • Core and shoulder muscles act as fixators, stabilising the torso and upper arm so the biceps can work effectively.
4. Analyse the knee joint action during the drive phase of a sprint start. +
Model answer points:
  • Joint: knee — a hinge joint between the femur and tibia.
  • Movement: extension.
  • Agonist: rectus femoris, contracting concentrically to extend the knee and drive the body forward.
  • Antagonist: biceps femoris, relaxing to allow extension.
  • Contraction type: concentric, since the agonist shortens while producing force.
