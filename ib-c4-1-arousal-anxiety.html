C.4.1 Theme C · Stress & Coping SL & HL

Arousal & Anxiety

How arousal and anxiety relate to performance: the inverted-U hypothesis, individual zones, the two components of anxiety and why high anxiety can cause a sudden collapse.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Arousal is the level of physical and psychological activation, from deep sleep to intense excitement. Anxiety is a negative emotional state with worry and physical symptoms. The two are related but different: arousal can be positive, and anxiety is the unpleasant form of activation. The inverted-U hypothesis proposes that performance improves with arousal up to an optimal point and then declines. The individual zone of optimal functioning (IZOF) recognises that the optimal point differs between athletes and between tasks. At higher level, anxiety is split into cognitive and somatic components, and catastrophe theory explains why performance can collapse suddenly when both are high.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Define arousal as physical and psychological activation, and distinguish it from anxiety.
  • Distinguish between cognitive and somatic anxiety, with examples.
  • Distinguish between trait and state anxiety.
  • Explain the inverted-U hypothesis, including the effect of task complexity, and the concept of IZOF.
  • HL: Explain catastrophe theory, and compare it with the inverted-U hypothesis and IZOF.

Key Terms

Deep Dive

Arousal: physical and psychological activation

Arousal has a physical side (heart rate, breathing, muscle tension, adrenaline release) and a psychological side (alertness, focus, readiness). It is controlled largely by the sympathetic nervous system and hormones such as adrenaline. Low arousal may leave an athlete flat and inattentive; high arousal may bring energy and readiness, but also tension. Arousal is not bad in itself. The question for performance is how much arousal there is compared with what the task needs.

Anxiety: cognitive and somatic components

Anxiety is more than high arousal. It has two components that are often measured separately. Cognitive anxiety is the worry, negative thinking and fear of failure. Somatic anxiety is the physical experience of arousal that is interpreted as unpleasant, such as a racing heart, sweaty palms, butterflies or muscle tension. An athlete can have high physical arousal without anxiety, for example when excited, and anxiety can be present with little physical arousal.

The components of anxiety: cognitive and somatic anxiety, and trait and state anxiety, with examples. Trait anxiety is a stable tendency; state anxiety is a temporary response to a situation.

Trait and state anxiety

Trait anxiety is a stable personality tendency to perceive situations as threatening and to feel anxious across many situations. State anxiety is a temporary emotional state in a particular situation, which can rise and fall quickly, for example in the minutes before a final. A high-trait athlete is more likely to experience high state anxiety, but the two are not the same. Coaches and sport psychologists often measure state anxiety just before competition with questionnaires such as the Competitive State Anxiety Inventory-2 (CSAI-2R), which assess cognitive anxiety, somatic anxiety and self-confidence.

The inverted-U hypothesis and IZOF

The inverted-U hypothesis proposes that performance increases as arousal increases, up to an optimal level, and then decreases as arousal becomes too high. Two points are important. First, the optimal level depends on the task: simple or gross motor tasks, such as a sprint start or a weightlift, tolerate and often need higher arousal, while complex or fine-motor tasks, such as a golf putt or a gymnastics balance, need lower arousal, because too much arousal disrupts precise control and attention. Second, the optimal point differs between athletes. The individual zone of optimal functioning (IZOF) proposes that each athlete has their own zone of arousal in which they perform best, and that this zone can be identified and used in training.

The inverted-U hypothesis for simple and complex tasks (schematic), with the individual zone of optimal functioning for one athlete shaded. The optimal arousal level is lower for complex tasks.

The model links to attention (see C.2.2). Arousal beyond the optimal level tends to narrow attention, so an athlete may focus on the threat and miss relevant cues. Coaches can use IZOF to help athletes recognise their own signs of too much or too little arousal, and then use strategies to adjust it.

Catastrophe theory HL

The inverted-U hypothesis predicts a gradual fall in performance after the optimal point. Catastrophe theory predicts something different when anxiety is involved. When cognitive anxiety is low, performance declines gradually as arousal rises past the optimal point, as the inverted-U predicts. When cognitive anxiety is high, performance may fall suddenly and severely once arousal passes a threshold, because the athlete's worry and physical arousal combine and the performance can collapse. This is why catastrophe theory explains the phrase "choking" under pressure.

Catastrophe theory compared with the inverted-U hypothesis (schematic). With low cognitive anxiety performance declines gradually; with high cognitive anxiety it drops suddenly once arousal passes the threshold.

Comparing the theories HL

Each theory explains a different part of the relationship:

TheoryKey ideaPrediction for high arousalLimitation
Drive theoryArousal increases the dominant response; skilled, well-learned responses improve while novel ones worsenPerformance improves for well-learned skills, worsens for new onesDoes not describe the optimal point clearly, and the drive-performance link is hard to test
Inverted-U hypothesisPerformance is best at a moderate, optimal arousal level, and the optimal level depends on task complexityGradual decline after the optimal pointOne optimal level is assumed for all athletes; the shape is hard to measure precisely
IZOFEach athlete has a personal zone of optimal arousalDecline beyond the athlete's own zoneZones are hard to identify reliably, and can change over time
Catastrophe theoryPerformance can collapse suddenly when cognitive and somatic anxiety are both highSudden, severe drop once a threshold is passedComplex to test; the threshold is hard to define

A balanced evaluation shows that these theories are not in competition. Inverted-U and IZOF describe the basic relationship, and catastrophe theory explains when performance can fall sharply. The best approach for coaches is to combine them: identify each athlete's zone, and watch for cognitive anxiety, which can make the drop sudden.

Exam Tips

📈
Name the optimal pointDon't describe the inverted-U without naming the optimal arousal level and the decline after it. Say whether the task is simple or complex, because this changes the optimal level.
🧩
Separate arousal from anxietyArousal is activation; anxiety is the unpleasant, worried form of it. Give one cognitive and one somatic example for anxiety. Check the IB command terms.
⚡
Catastrophe theory needs both componentsDon't explain it as "high arousal only". It requires high cognitive anxiety combined with high somatic arousal, which causes a sudden drop.
⚖️
Evaluate the theories togetherFor comparison questions, state what each theory predicts for high arousal and one limitation of each.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't say that all athletes have the same optimal arousal level, or that low arousal is always the best way to control anxiety. The optimal point differs between athletes and tasks.

Model answer

  • Beyond this point, performance declines as arousal becomes too high.

5(HL) Explain how catastrophe theory explains a sudden fall in performance under pressure.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Catastrophe theory proposes that performance can fall suddenly and severely when arousal passes a threshold.
  • This happens when cognitive anxiety (worry, fear of failure) is high alongside high somatic anxiety (physical arousal).
  • With low cognitive anxiety, performance declines more gradually, as the inverted-U predicts.
  • An athlete with high cognitive and somatic anxiety at a penalty may therefore suffer a sudden breakdown, which is sometimes described as choking.
