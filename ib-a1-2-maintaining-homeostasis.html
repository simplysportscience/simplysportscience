A.1.2 Theme A · Communication SL & HL

Maintaining Homeostasis

How the body keeps its internal conditions stable, even when exercise pushes body temperature and blood glucose away from normal.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Homeostasis is the body keeping its internal environment stable, things like core temperature, blood glucose and water levels. Exercise constantly disturbs these conditions. Working muscles produce heat and use up glucose. The body detects these changes and corrects them, mainly through negative feedback, using the nervous and endocrine systems you met in A.1.1.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Define homeostasis.
  • Outline the parts of a negative feedback loop.
  • Explain how core temperature is regulated during exercise.
  • Describe the four ways the body loses heat.
  • Explain how insulin and glucagon regulate blood glucose around exercise.

Key Terms

HomeostasisKeeping the body's internal environment stable.
Negative feedbackA change triggers a response that reverses that change.
ReceptorDetects a change in a condition, e.g. temperature.
Control centreCompares the change to normal and sends a response, e.g. the hypothalamus.
EffectorCarries out the response, e.g. sweat glands or blood vessels.
ThermoregulationKeeping core body temperature stable, at about 37 °C.

Deep Dive

Negative feedback: the body's correction system

Most homeostasis works through a negative feedback loop. When a condition moves away from its normal level, the body responds in a way that pushes it back. Every loop has the same parts:

  • Stimulus: a change from normal, e.g. core temperature rises during a run.
  • Receptors: detect the change, e.g. thermoreceptors in the skin and hypothalamus.
  • Control centre: compares the change with the normal level and sends a response, e.g. the hypothalamus.
  • Effectors: carry out the response, e.g. sweat glands and skin blood vessels.
  • Response: the condition returns towards normal, and the loop switches off.
Negative feedback during exercise: a rise in core temperature is detected, processed and then reversed.

Thermoregulation during exercise

Muscles are inefficient. Around three quarters of the energy they use is released as heat, so core temperature rises quickly during exercise. The hypothalamus acts as the body's thermostat and triggers two main cooling responses:

  • Vasodilation of skin blood vessels: more warm blood flows near the skin surface, where heat can escape.
  • Sweating: sweat glands release sweat, and as it evaporates it carries heat away from the skin.

The body loses heat in four ways:

MechanismHow it worksSporting example
EvaporationSweat turns to vapour, removing heat. This is the main method during exercise.A marathon runner sweating heavily
ConvectionMoving air or water carries heat away from the skin.A cyclist cooled by the airflow
ConductionHeat passes through direct contact with something cooler.A cold towel or ice vest at half-time
RadiationHeat is given off as infrared energy to cooler surroundings.Heat radiating from the skin in the shade

Exercising in the heat is harder. When the air is hot, radiation and convection work less well, or even add heat to the body. In humid conditions, sweat can't evaporate easily, so it drips off without cooling you. More blood is sent to the skin, leaving less for working muscles, and heavy sweating causes dehydration (see A.2.1).

Blood glucose regulation

Blood glucose is controlled by two hormones from the pancreas, working in opposite directions:

Insulin and glucagon act in opposite directions to keep blood glucose within its normal range.
InsulinGlucagon
Released whenBlood glucose is too high, e.g. after a mealBlood glucose is too low, e.g. during long exercise
Main effectCells take in glucose and the liver stores it as glycogenThe liver breaks glycogen down and releases glucose into the blood
ResultBlood glucose fallsBlood glucose rises

During exercise, muscles take up glucose rapidly. Insulin release falls, while glucagon and adrenaline rise. Together they stimulate the liver to break down glycogen, which keeps blood glucose stable so the brain and muscles stay fuelled. If liver glycogen runs out in a long event, blood glucose can drop and performance suffers. This is one reason endurance athletes take on carbohydrate during races.

Exam Tips

🔁
Use the loop languageFor feedback questions, name each part in order: stimulus, receptor, control centre, effector, response. Each step is usually worth a mark.
🌡️
Explain how, not just what"Sweating cools you" is incomplete. Say that evaporation of sweat removes heat from the skin.
💧
Link heat and hydrationQuestions on exercising in the heat often reward linking sweating to fluid loss, reduced blood volume and lower performance.
🏃
Always add a sporting exampleAnchor your answer in a real situation, such as a marathon in hot, humid conditions.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't mix up glucagon and glycogen. Glucagon is the hormone, and glycogen is the stored carbohydrate it breaks down.

5Explain how the body maintains core temperature during a marathon in hot conditions.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
