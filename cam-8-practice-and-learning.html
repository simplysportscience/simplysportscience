Topic 8 Theme 2 · Skill, learning and practice AS Level 8386

Practice and Learning

Types of practice, types of guidance, the stages of learning, transfer of learning and motivation.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

How a skill is practised has a major effect on how well it is learned. A coach must choose the type of practice, the type of guidance and the feedback that suit the learner’s stage of learning, and must also consider how the skill will transfer to other situations. This topic asks you to explain each approach and to evaluate its advantages and disadvantages.

Motivation links the whole topic, because learners who are motivated practise more and learn more effectively.

Key Terms

Whole practiceThe skill is practised as a complete action.
Massed practiceRepeated practice with little or no rest between attempts.
Distributed practicePractice spread out, with rest periods between attempts or sessions.
Positive transferLearning one skill helps the learning of another.
Negative transferLearning one skill hinders the learning of another.
Intrinsic motivationMotivation that comes from within the performer, such as enjoyment.
Extrinsic motivationMotivation from outside the performer, through tangible rewards (such as medals) or intangible rewards (such as praise).

Deep Dive

8.1 Types of practice

Whole practice performs the skill as one action. It suits skills that are continuous or have high organisation, because the parts cannot easily be separated. It can be overwhelming for complex skills, because the learner must attend to everything at once.

Part practice breaks the skill into parts and practises each separately. It suits skills that are serial or have low organisation. Parts may be practised out of context, so the skill may not come together at the end.

Whole–part–whole practice combines both: the whole skill is performed, then the difficult part is practised, then the whole skill is performed again. It gives the learner a sense of the whole, and targets the difficult part.

Progressive part practice starts with one part and adds the next part in sequence, until the whole skill is built up. It suits serial skills with high organisation.

Massed practice has short rest periods; distributed practice has longer rests between attempts or sessions. Massed practice gives more repetitions in less time, but can cause fatigue and lower retention. Distributed practice usually gives better retention and long-term learning.

Fixed practice uses the same conditions each time, which helps the learner build a consistent movement pattern. Varied practice changes the conditions, which helps the learner adapt and supports transfer to new situations.

Evaluate each type by giving one advantage and one disadvantage, and by stating the type of skill and the stage of learning for which it suits best.

8.2 Types of guidance

Visual guidance uses demonstrations, video or images to show the skill. It is effective for beginners, but a learner may copy the surface of the movement without understanding its key features.

Verbal guidance uses instructions, cues and feedback given in words. It helps the learner understand the purpose of the movement, but it can overload a learner in the early stages of learning.

Manual guidance involves the teacher physically moving or supporting the learner. It gives the learner a feel for the correct movement, but it can make the learner dependent on support and may not transfer when the support is removed.

Mechanical guidance uses equipment to support or direct the movement, such as a harness or a floating board. It reduces risk and allows the learner to attempt the movement, but the learner may depend on the equipment.

Evaluate guidance by stating the advantage for the learner and the risk of over-reliance.

8.3 Stages of learning

Learners move through three stages, which Fitts and Posner described. In the cognitive stage, the learner understands what the skill involves, makes many errors and relies on verbal and visual guidance and on whole or part practice. In the associative stage, errors reduce and the learner refines the movement, using knowledge of performance to correct it. In the autonomous stage, the skill becomes automatic, so attention can be given to other things such as tactics, and the performer uses intrinsic feedback.

When you evaluate the use of feedback, practice or guidance at each stage, link the choice to the learner’s needs. For example, extrinsic knowledge of results helps a cognitive learner understand the outcome, while varied practice helps an autonomous performer adapt to changing situations.

8.4 Transfer of learning

Transfer of learning is the effect of learning one skill on the learning of another. It can be positive (helpful), negative (harmful) or zero (no effect). Proactive transfer is when an earlier skill affects later learning; retroactive transfer is when later learning affects an earlier skill. Bilateral transfer is the transfer of learning from one limb or side of the body to the other.

To optimise positive transfer, a coach can emphasise the common elements between skills, teach the underlying principles, and use varied practice. To limit negative transfer, a coach can highlight the differences between the skills, practise the two skills separately at first, and use gradual progression.

8.5 Motivation

Intrinsic motivation comes from within the performer, from enjoyment, interest or a sense of achievement. It usually supports long-term participation and effort. Extrinsic motivation comes from outside, through tangible rewards (medals, money, equipment) or intangible rewards (praise, recognition, status).

When you evaluate the types of motivation, consider the performer and the context. Extrinsic rewards can encourage a beginner to try a skill, but intrinsic motivation is usually more important for sustained practice. A reward that is controlling, such as a payment for winning, may reduce the performer’s enjoyment of the activity.

Exam Tips

🎯
Exam tipWhen you evaluate a type of practice, give both an advantage and a disadvantage, and name the type of skill it suits.
🧠
Exam tipFor guidance, name the type and link it to the stage of learning: visual and verbal guidance dominate early learning, and manual guidance is usually reduced later.
📐
Exam tipFor transfer, use the exact terms (positive, negative, zero, proactive, retroactive, bilateral) and give an example for each one you use.
🔁
Exam tipFor motivation, keep tangible and intangible rewards separate, and say which type a coach is using in your example.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1Explain the difference between massed and distributed practice.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Massed practice has short rest periods between repetitions, while distributed practice has longer rest periods.
  • Massed practice gives more repetitions in less time, but distributed practice usually gives better retention.
2Evaluate the use of whole–part–whole practice for a learner in the associative stage.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The whole skill is performed, giving the learner a sense of the whole action.
  • The difficult part is then practised, which targets the element that is causing errors.
  • The whole skill is performed again, so the part is placed back in context.
  • The method suits the associative stage, although it takes more time than whole practice alone.
3Explain how a coach could optimise positive transfer between the forehand in tennis and the forehand in squash.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Emphasise the common elements, such as the grip, the shoulder rotation and the timing of the swing.
  • Teach the underlying principles of the stroke, which apply to both sports.
  • Use varied practice so the learner can adapt the stroke to different situations.
4Explain the difference between tangible and intangible rewards, and give one example of each.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Tangible rewards are physical, such as medals or prize money.
  • Intangible rewards are non-physical, such as praise or recognition.
