A.3.2 Theme A · Response SL & HL

Benefits to Health of Being Active

How regular physical activity protects health and well-being, what it does to the muscular and immune systems, and why inactivity carries real risks.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Being active is one of the most effective things a person can do for their health. Regular physical activity improves physical, mental and social well-being, strengthens the muscular system, supports the immune system and lowers the risk of non-communicable diseases such as heart disease, type 2 diabetes and osteoporosis. Inactivity does the opposite. How active people are depends on many determinants: personal, social, environmental and economic. At higher level, you also study exercise prescription: how activity is planned for different people and conditions.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Explain how physical activity supports physical, mental and social well-being.
  • Outline physical activity guidelines and the determinants of activity levels.
  • Describe the effects of regular physical activity on the muscular and immune systems.
  • Explain the health risks of inactivity and how activity protects against them.
  • HL: Explain the principles and purposes of exercise prescription.
  • HL: Apply exercise prescription to different target groups and conditions.

Key Terms

Physical activityAny movement by skeletal muscles that uses energy, including exercise, sport, play and active travel.
Sedentary behaviourLow-energy sitting or lying while awake, such as long periods at a desk or screen.
Well-beingA person's overall physical, mental and social health and quality of life.
Non-communicable disease (NCD)A long-term disease that does not spread between people, such as heart disease or type 2 diabetes.
Insulin sensitivityHow effectively the body's cells respond to insulin and take up glucose.
Exercise prescription (HL)An individual plan of activity (type, amount, intensity and progression) to meet a health or fitness goal.

Deep Dive

Physical activity and well-being

Physical activity is any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that uses energy. Exercise is a planned, structured form of it. Sedentary behaviour (long periods of sitting) is a separate health risk, so a person who exercises for 30 minutes but sits for the rest of the day is still at some risk. Being active supports three areas of well-being:

  • Physical: a fitter heart, lungs, muscles and bones, a healthier body composition and better sleep.
  • Mental: lower stress, anxiety and depression, better mood and self-esteem, and improved concentration and thinking.
  • Social: team sport, clubs and active travel build friendships, a sense of belonging and support.

How much activity is enough?

The World Health Organization (WHO) gives these guidelines for health:

GroupAerobic activityStrength and other activity
Children and adolescents (5–17)An average of at least 60 minutes a day of moderate to vigorous activity, mostly aerobicVigorous aerobic activity and muscle- and bone-strengthening activity on at least 3 days a week
Adults (18–64)150–300 minutes of moderate activity, or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity, per week (or an equivalent mix)Muscle-strengthening activity at moderate or greater intensity on at least 2 days a week
Older adults (65+)The same as adults, as far as health allowsAlso multicomponent activity with balance and strength training on at least 3 days a week

All groups should also limit sedentary time. Vigorous activity counts for roughly twice as much as moderate activity, so 10 minutes of running is about equal to 20 minutes of brisk walking. A useful check is the talk test: you can talk but not sing during moderate activity, and you can only say a few words during vigorous activity.

The shape of the relationship between weekly activity and the risk of early death, based on population studies. The values are approximate and the curve is illustrative. Some activity is better than none, and more is generally better up to a point.

Effects on the muscular system

Regular activity changes muscle in ways that depend on the type of training:

  • Resistance training: increases muscle size (hypertrophy) and strength, partly through improved nervous system activation of muscle fibres. It also strengthens tendons and ligaments.
  • Endurance training: increases capillaries and mitochondria in muscle, so more oxygen can be delivered and used, and improves the use of fat as a fuel.
  • All types: improve insulin sensitivity and glucose uptake, balance and coordination.
  • Across life: keeping muscle mass and strength reduces the age-related loss of muscle (sarcopenia), reduces the risk of falls and helps people stay independent.

Effects on the immune system

The immune system defends the body against infection. Regular, moderate activity strengthens it: immune cells circulate more during and after exercise, so the body is better at detecting threats, and long-term activity reduces chronic low-grade inflammation (partly by reducing body fat). People who are regularly active tend to catch fewer upper respiratory infections such as colds.

The relationship is shown by the "J-curve" model. Very heavy training with too little rest, sleep or fuel can temporarily suppress immune function after exercise, so athletes in the middle of heavy blocks of training, or after marathons, may be more likely to pick up infections.

The J-curve model of training load and infection risk. It is a simplified model: the lowest risk is at regular moderate activity, and very heavy load without recovery can raise risk above the sedentary level.

Health risks of inactivity, and how activity protects

Physical inactivity is a leading risk factor for non-communicable diseases. Adults who do not meet the guidelines have around a 20–30% higher risk of death than those who do. Activity protects health through specific effects on body systems:

Pathways from regular activity to the effects on body systems and the health outcomes. In exams, link activity to the physiological effect and then to the outcome.
ConditionWhy inactivity raises the riskHow activity protects
Cardiovascular disease and hypertensionLess demand on the heart, higher blood pressure, unhealthy blood fats, narrower arteriesStronger heart, lower resting blood pressure, improved blood fats and healthier blood vessels
Type 2 diabetesMuscles take up less glucose and insulin sensitivity falls, especially with excess body fatContracting muscles take up glucose and become more insulin sensitive, and body fat falls
ObesityEnergy intake exceeds energy usedIncreases energy expenditure and helps maintain muscle mass, so supports healthy body composition
OsteoporosisBones that are not loaded lose densityWeight-bearing and resistance exercise stimulate bone-building, raising peak bone mass and slowing loss
Some cancers (for example colon and breast)Linked to excess body fat, hormone levels and inflammationLowers body fat, inflammation and some hormone levels
Depression and anxietyLess stress relief, poorer sleep, less social contactChanges brain chemistry, improves sleep and self-esteem, and offers social contact

Exercise prescription: principles and purposes HL

Exercise prescription is an individual plan of activity designed to reach a goal. Its purposes include improving health and fitness, preventing and managing disease, supporting rehabilitation and improving performance. Good prescription:

  • Starts with screening: health history, current activity, medication and risk factors, with medical clearance where needed.
  • Is individual: it considers age, fitness, health, goals, preferences and what the person can keep doing.
  • Sets the FITT-VP variables: frequency, intensity, time (duration), type, volume and progression, and chooses a safe starting level.
  • Progresses gradually and is monitored: intensity can be measured by heart rate, perceived exertion (RPE) or the talk test, and the plan is adjusted.
  • Supports adherence: enjoyable, realistic activity with support is more likely to continue. Poor prescription can cause injury, overuse, dropout or little benefit.

Exercise prescription for different groups and conditions HL

Group or conditionKey considerations
Children and adolescentsAim for about 60 minutes a day of varied, enjoyable activity and play, including bone- and muscle-strengthening on at least 3 days a week. Supervise strength training and technique.
Older adultsCombine aerobic, strength and balance training to reduce falls, preserve muscle and bone, and keep independence. Start gently and allow more recovery.
PregnancyRegular moderate activity is generally recommended with medical advice. Avoid contact or fall-risk activity and overheating, and adjust as pregnancy progresses.
Overweight and obesityBuild up gradually, using lower-impact activities that protect the joints, and combine with diet changes. More time per week helps weight management.
Type 2 diabetesCombine aerobic and resistance exercise on most days, and monitor blood glucose and its link with food and medication.
HypertensionRegular moderate aerobic activity lowers blood pressure. Avoid heavy lifting with breath-holding, which sharply raises blood pressure.
Established heart diseaseStart with medical clearance and supervision, at low to moderate intensity, and progress carefully.

Exam Tips

🔗
Link activity → effect → outcomeDon't just list benefits. Write the chain: "Weight-bearing exercise stimulates bone-building, which raises bone density and lowers the risk of osteoporosis."
🔢
Use the guideline numbersQuote them: adults need 150–300 minutes of moderate (or 75–150 minutes of vigorous) activity a week, with muscle-strengthening on at least 2 days. Numbers show precise knowledge.
🗂️
Classify determinantsSort factors into personal, social, environmental and economic, and give a specific example of each. Check the IB command terms.
🏃
For HL prescription questions, name the group's needSay why the plan differs: older adults need balance work to prevent falls, and people with hypertension should avoid breath-holding heavy lifts.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't write that "more exercise is always better for health". Gains get smaller at higher amounts, and very high loads with poor recovery can increase injury and infection risk. Moderate, regular activity is the key message.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1State the minimum amount of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week recommended for adults by the World Health Organization.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • 150 minutes per week (the recommended range is 150–300 minutes of moderate activity, or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity).
2Outline two environmental determinants of physical activity levels.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Access to safe parks, sports facilities and walking or cycling routes.
  • Climate and weather, such as extreme heat or cold, which can reduce outdoor activity.
  • Neighbourhood design and safety, for example how walkable it is, traffic, lighting or air pollution.
  • Any two.
3(HL) Outline three factors to consider when prescribing exercise for an older adult.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Health screening: medical history, medication and any conditions, with medical clearance where needed.
  • Include balance and strength training as well as aerobic activity, to reduce the risk of falls and preserve muscle and bone.
  • Start at a low intensity and progress gradually, allowing longer recovery between sessions.
  • Any three. Enjoyment, social support and joint health are also accepted.
4Explain how regular physical activity reduces the risk of type 2 diabetes.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Muscle contraction increases glucose uptake into muscle cells, which lowers blood glucose.
  • Regular activity increases insulin sensitivity, so cells respond better to insulin and less insulin is needed.
  • It reduces body fat, especially around the abdomen, which reduces insulin resistance.
5Explain the relationship between training load and the risk of infection.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The relationship is J-shaped: infection risk is lowest with regular moderate activity.
  • Regular moderate activity improves immune function, for example by improving immune surveillance and reducing chronic inflammation, so the risk of infection is lower than for a sedentary person.
  • Very heavy training loads with too little recovery, sleep or fuel can temporarily suppress immune function, so the risk of infection rises.
  • At very high loads the risk may exceed that of a sedentary person; adequate recovery and nutrition reduce this.
