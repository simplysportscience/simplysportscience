Skill and Ability

How skills are classified along six continua, and what abilities are and how they underpin skilled performance.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Before you can explain how a skill is learned, you need to describe what the skill is. Skills are classified using continua: each is a scale between two extremes, and a skill is placed on each scale to show where it sits. Classification is the first step in deciding how to teach a skill and which practice methods will suit it.

Skill is linked to ability, which is the underlying capacity a performer brings to a task. Abilities are innate, underpinning and enduring, and they shape how easily a person acquires new skills.

Key Terms

Fine skillA skill that depends mainly on small, precise muscle movements, such as a golf putt.
Gross skillA skill that depends mainly on large muscle groups, such as a sprint start.
Open skillA skill performed in an unpredictable environment that changes, such as tackling in rugby.
Closed skillA skill performed in a predictable environment, with no outside influence, such as a free throw.
Discrete skillA skill with a clear beginning and end, such as a penalty kick.
AbilityA relatively stable, innate capacity that underpins the learning of skills.

Deep Dive

5.1 Classification of skills

Skills are placed on six continua. Each continuum describes a different feature of the skill, so a single skill can sit at different points on each one:

  • Muscular involvement: fine (small, precise movements) to gross (large movements of major muscle groups).
  • Environmental influence: open (the environment changes and must be read) to closed (the environment is predictable).
  • Continuity: discrete (clear start and finish) to serial (discrete actions linked in sequence) to continuous (no clear start or finish, such as swimming).
  • Pacing: externally paced (timed by the environment, such as a return of serve) to internally paced (timed by the performer, such as a golf swing).
  • Difficulty: basic (simple, few components) to complex (many components that must be coordinated).
  • Organisation: high organisation (a sequence of closely linked components that must follow a precise order) to low organisation (components that can be performed in a looser order).

To justify a classification, place the skill on each continuum and give a reason from the activity itself. For example, a netball shot from a set position is fine (precise finger and wrist control), closed (the defence is not moving), discrete, internally paced, and of moderate organisation.

5.2 Abilities

An ability is a relatively stable, underlying capacity. The specification describes three characteristics:

  • Innate: abilities are partly inherited, so performers start with different levels of each ability.
  • Underpinning: abilities form the base on which skills are built. For example, hand–eye coordination underpins many ball skills.
  • Enduring: abilities change slowly and are relatively stable across a person’s life, so they are not easily altered by a short period of practice.

Distinguish clearly between an ability and a skill. A skill is learned and specific to a task; an ability is a general capacity that supports many skills. A performer may have a high level of reaction ability but a low level of skill in a particular sport, because they have not yet learned the specific movement patterns.

Exam Tips

🎯
Exam tipUse all six continua when you justify a classification. Examiners reward a complete justification, not a single continuum.
🧠
Exam tipGive a reason for each placement that refers to the activity: "closed, because the environment is predictable" is a complete point.
📐
Exam tipKeep ability and skill separate in your answer, and use "underpinning" to show how the two are linked.
🔁
Exam tipUse a sporting example for every continuum you describe, and make sure it fits the continuum you are describing.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1Classify a basketball free throw on the continua of muscular involvement, environmental influence and pacing. Justify each classification.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Muscular involvement: fine, because the shot depends on precise hand and wrist control.
  • Environmental influence: closed, because the shooter is not reacting to a changing environment.
  • Pacing: internally paced, because the shooter decides when to start the shot.
2Explain what is meant by an ability, and explain how an ability differs from a skill.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • An ability is a relatively stable, underlying capacity that supports the learning of many skills.
  • A skill is learned and specific to a particular task.
  • For example, reaction ability is general, while the technique used in a specific sport is a learned skill.
3Explain why a tennis rally is classified as open rather than closed.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The environment is constantly changing, because the opponent’s shots vary in speed, direction and placement.
  • The performer must read and respond to these changes.
