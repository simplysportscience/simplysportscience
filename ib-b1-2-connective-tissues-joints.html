B.1.2 Theme B · Generating Movement in the Body SL & HL

Structure & Function of Connective Tissues & Joints

The tissues that link muscle to bone and bone to bone, and the joints that trade off movement against stability.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Movement needs more than muscles and bones. Connective tissues link and support everything: tendons join muscle to bone, ligaments join bone to bone, cartilage cushions and smooths joint surfaces, and fascia wraps and connects muscles. Where bones meet, joints can be fibrous (fixed), cartilaginous (slightly movable) or synovial (freely movable). Each design is a trade-off: the more freedom a joint has, the more it relies on ligaments and muscles to keep it stable.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Describe the structure and function of the main connective tissues: tendons, ligaments, cartilage, bone and fascia.
  • Distinguish between fibrous, cartilaginous and synovial joints.
  • Describe the structure of a synovial joint and the function of each part.
  • Name the classes of synovial joint, the movements each allows and an example of each.
  • Explain the factors that affect joint stability and how connective tissues work together in movement.

Key Terms

Connective tissueTissue that supports, connects and protects other tissues, made of cells in a fibre-rich matrix.
TendonA strong cord of connective tissue joining muscle to bone.
LigamentA strong band of connective tissue joining bone to bone and stabilising a joint.
Articular cartilageSmooth cartilage covering the ends of bones at a synovial joint.
Synovial fluidThe lubricating fluid in a synovial joint cavity.
Synovial jointA freely movable joint with a fluid-filled cavity between the bones.

Deep Dive

Connective tissues: structure and function

Connective tissues are made of cells scattered in an extracellular matrix. The matrix contains protein fibres: collagen gives strength when pulled (tensile strength), and elastin gives stretch and recoil. The arrangement of the fibres decides what each tissue is good at.

TissueStructureFunctionSporting example
TendonDense regular connective tissue: tightly packed collagen fibres lined up in parallel, with few blood vesselsJoins muscle to bone and transmits the force of muscle contraction to the bone; stores and releases some elastic energyThe Achilles tendon in running and jumping
LigamentDense regular connective tissue: collagen fibres in parallel, with some elastin; a little more flexible than tendon; few blood vesselsJoins bone to bone, stabilises joints and limits excessive or unwanted movementThe anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) in the knee when sidestepping
CartilageFirm, flexible tissue with collagen in a gel-like matrix; no blood supply. Hyaline cartilage covers joint surfaces; fibrocartilage is tougherReduces friction and absorbs shock; fibrocartilage forms discs and menisci that cushion jointsKnee menisci absorbing landing forces
BoneCollagen strengthened with calcium phosphate minerals, with a blood supply and marrowSupport, protection, levers for movement, mineral storage, blood cell productionThe femur transmitting forces in running
FasciaSheets of connective tissue wrapping muscles, groups of muscles and organsHolds muscles in place, reduces friction between them, and helps transmit forceThe thick fascia on the sole of the foot (plantar fascia)

Tendons, ligaments and cartilage have a poor blood supply, so they heal slowly after injury. They also adapt slowly to training, which is one reason why training load should increase gradually.

Structure of a synovial joint

Most of the joints used in sport are synovial. The bone ends are covered with smooth articular cartilage and enclosed in a capsule, with a fluid-filled space between them.

A simplified synovial joint. Cartilage covers the bone ends, the capsule encloses the joint, and ligaments and tendons add stability and movement.
  • Articular cartilage covers the bone ends, giving a smooth, low-friction surface that absorbs some shock.
  • Joint (synovial) cavity and synovial fluid: the fluid lubricates the joint and reduces friction, supplies nutrients to the cartilage (which has no blood supply of its own), absorbs shock and removes waste.
  • Synovial membrane: lines the capsule and makes the synovial fluid.
  • Fibrous capsule: a tough sleeve of connective tissue that encloses the joint and holds the bones together.
  • Ligaments: strengthen the capsule and stop movement beyond the normal range.
  • Other structures: menisci (fibrocartilage pads in the knee) deepen the joint and absorb shock, and bursae (small fluid-filled sacs) reduce friction between tendons, muscles and bone.

Classes of synovial joint

The shape of the bone ends decides how many ways a synovial joint can move.

The six classes of synovial joint. The knee is usually described as a modified hinge, because it also allows a small amount of rotation when bent.

Stability, mobility and training

There is a trade-off between mobility (range of movement) and stability (resistance to dislocation). The shoulder is a ball-and-socket joint with a shallow socket, so it has a huge range but is easily dislocated and relies on ligaments and the surrounding muscles (the rotator cuff). The hip has a deep socket and strong ligaments, so it is more stable but has a smaller range. Joint stability depends on:

  • the shape of the bone ends and how well they fit together;
  • ligaments and the joint capsule;
  • the muscles and tendons crossing the joint, which can be strengthened by training;
  • cartilage and fascia around the joint.

During an explosive movement such as a jump, these tissues work as a team: muscles contract and tendons transmit and store force, ligaments keep the joints in line, and cartilage and fascia absorb and spread the load on landing. Regular, gradually progressed training can increase the strength and stiffness of tendons and the strength of bone, and keeps cartilage healthy through moderate loading. A warm-up raises tissue temperature and makes it easier to stretch.

Exam Tips

🎯
Link structure to functionDon't just define. Say why it suits the job: "Tendons have parallel collagen fibres, so they resist large pulling forces from the muscle."
🧩
Learn the synovial joint parts and a job for eachCartilage reduces friction, synovial fluid lubricates and feeds the cartilage, the capsule encloses the joint, and ligaments stabilise it. Check the IB command terms.
🏃
Match joint class to movementName the joint class, the movements it allows and an example, such as "hinge joint at the elbow: flexion and extension only".
⚖️
Use the stability and mobility trade-offFor comparison questions (shoulder and hip, for instance), refer to the depth of the socket, ligaments and muscles.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't mix up tendons and ligaments: tendons join muscle to bone, ligaments join bone to bone. And don't describe every joint as synovial: some are fibrous or cartilaginous.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1State the function of a ligament.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • A ligament joins bone to bone, stabilising the joint and limiting excessive movement.
2Outline two functions of synovial fluid.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • It lubricates the joint, reducing friction between the cartilage surfaces.
  • It supplies nutrients to the articular cartilage and removes waste.
  • It helps absorb shock. Any two.
3Identify the class of synovial joint at the shoulder and at the elbow, and state the movement each allows.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The shoulder is a ball-and-socket joint.
  • This allows movement in all planes: flexion, extension, abduction, adduction and rotation (and circumduction).
  • The elbow is a hinge joint, allowing flexion and extension only.
4Explain why the shoulder joint is more mobile but less stable than the hip joint.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The shoulder socket is shallow and the head of the humerus is large relative to it, allowing a wide range of movement.
  • The hip socket is deep and the ball fits tightly, with strong ligaments, so the hip is more stable but has a smaller range.
  • The shoulder therefore relies more on muscles, tendons and ligaments (the rotator cuff) for stability and is more prone to dislocation.
5Discuss how connective tissues work together to support joint stability and efficiency during an explosive movement such as a jump.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Tendons transmit the force of muscle contraction to the bones to produce movement, and can store and release elastic energy to improve jump efficiency.
  • Ligaments hold the bones in position and limit excessive movement, especially on landing.
  • Articular cartilage and menisci reduce friction and absorb shock during take-off and landing.
  • Fascia transmits force between muscles and supports them; together these tissues protect the joint and allow force to be produced and absorbed safely.
