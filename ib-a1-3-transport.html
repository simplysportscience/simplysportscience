A.1.3 Theme A · Communication SL & HL

Transport

How the heart, blood vessels and lungs work together to deliver oxygen and nutrients, remove carbon dioxide and heat, and meet the rising demands of exercise.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Every cell in your body needs a delivery service. The cardiovascular system (heart, blood and blood vessels) pumps blood around the body. The respiratory system (airways and lungs) brings oxygen in and removes carbon dioxide. Together they transport oxygen, nutrients, hormones, waste products and heat. During exercise the demand rises, so heart rate, stroke volume, blood pressure, blood flow and breathing all change. The size of those changes depends on intensity, fitness, age, sex, body size and the type of activity.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Describe the path of blood through the heart, lungs and body.
  • Define heart rate, stroke volume, cardiac output, blood pressure, tidal volume, respiration rate and minute ventilation.
  • Explain how each of these variables changes as exercise intensity rises.
  • Explain how blood is redistributed during exercise, and how blood carries heat to the skin.
  • Outline how gases are exchanged in the lungs and muscles and transported in the blood.
  • Explain how age, sex, body size, fitness and type of activity affect these variables.

Key Terms

Cardiac output (Q)The volume of blood pumped by the heart each minute: Q = HR × SV.
Stroke volume (SV)The volume of blood pumped out of the heart in one beat.
Blood pressure (BP)The force of blood against the walls of the arteries, written as systolic/diastolic.
Blood redistributionChanging the share of cardiac output sent to each organ, by vasodilation and vasoconstriction.
Tidal volume (TV)The volume of air breathed in or out in one breath.
Minute ventilation (VE)The volume of air breathed per minute: VE = TV × respiration rate.

Deep Dive

Why the body needs a transport system

Working muscles use oxygen and fuel quickly, and they produce carbon dioxide, waste products and heat. Diffusion alone is far too slow to move these over the distances involved, so the body uses the bloodstream as a delivery and collection service. The heart is the pump, the blood vessels are the pipes, and the lungs are where oxygen enters and carbon dioxide leaves.

The heart and the two circuits

The heart is really two pumps side by side. The right side pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs (the pulmonary circuit). The left side pumps oxygenated blood to the rest of the body (the systemic circuit). Valves between the chambers and at the exits make sure blood only flows one way. The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it must generate enough pressure to push blood around the whole body.

Heart rate, stroke volume and cardiac output

Heart rate (HR) is the number of beats per minute. Stroke volume (SV) is the volume of blood pumped out of the heart in one beat. Cardiac output (Q) is the volume pumped per minute:

Q = HR × SV

At rest, a typical adult has a heart rate of about 70 beats per minute and a stroke volume of about 70 mL, giving a cardiac output of about 5 L per minute. As exercise intensity increases:

  • Heart rate rises in a near-straight line with intensity. It starts to rise even before exercise begins (the anticipatory rise), driven by the sympathetic nervous system and adrenaline (see A.1.1).
  • Stroke volume rises at first, then levels off at moderate to high intensity. It rises because more blood returns to the heart (the muscle pump and respiratory pump squeeze the veins, and veins constrict), so the ventricle fills more and contracts harder. Sympathetic stimulation also makes each contraction more forceful. It levels off because at very high heart rates there is less time to fill between beats.
  • Cardiac output therefore rises sharply, first because both HR and SV increase, and later mainly because HR keeps increasing.
Typical response of an untrained adult. Heart rate climbs steadily, stroke volume levels off, and cardiac output keeps rising because Q = HR × SV.

Stroke volume (mL)about 70about 110about 200

Notice that the elite athlete's maximum heart rate is no higher than anyone else's. Their much larger maximum cardiac output comes from a much larger stroke volume, because endurance training enlarges the heart's chambers and increases blood volume. This is also why trained athletes have a lower resting heart rate: each beat moves more blood, so fewer beats are needed to give the same resting cardiac output.

Blood pressure

Blood pressure is the force of blood against the artery walls. It is written as systolic pressure (when the ventricles contract) over diastolic pressure (when they relax). A typical resting value is about 120/80 mmHg.

  • Dynamic exercise (running, cycling, swimming): systolic pressure rises with intensity because cardiac output rises. Diastolic pressure stays about the same, or falls slightly, because the arterioles in the working muscles dilate and lower resistance.
  • Resistance exercise (a heavy squat or deadlift): both systolic and diastolic pressure can rise sharply, especially if the lifter holds their breath (the Valsalva manoeuvre). This is why very heavy lifting is more demanding on the cardiovascular system than its energy cost suggests.

Redistribution of blood flow

Even at maximal effort the heart can only pump so much blood, so the body also changes where the blood goes. At rest, muscles receive only about 20% of cardiac output. In heavy exercise this rises to about 80%, while the digestive system and kidneys receive much less.

  • Vasoconstriction: sympathetic nerves narrow the arterioles supplying the digestive system and kidneys, so less blood reaches them.
  • Vasodilation: in the working muscles, rising carbon dioxide, hydrogen ions (falling pH) and heat make the arterioles widen and open more capillaries, so far more blood flows through.
  • Brain and heart: flow is maintained or increased, because these organs cannot go without oxygen.
  • Heat transport: blood carries heat from the working muscles to the skin, where skin arterioles dilate so heat can be lost (see A.1.2).

Pulmonary ventilation

Tidal volume (TV) is the volume of air in one breath. Respiration rate (RR) is the number of breaths per minute. Minute ventilation (VE) is the volume of air moved per minute:

VE = TV × RR

At rest, a typical adult breathes about 0.5 L per breath, 12 times per minute, so VE is about 6 L per minute. In maximal exercise, tidal volume can reach 2.5 L or more and respiration rate can reach 40 to 60 breaths per minute, so VE can exceed 100 L per minute. Tidal volume levels off at moderate to high intensity, so at the highest intensities most of the extra ventilation comes from breathing faster.

Typical ventilation response of an untrained adult. Tidal volume levels off, respiration rate keeps rising, and minute ventilation increases roughly twenty-fold.

At rest, breathing in is driven by the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles, and breathing out is passive. During exercise, extra muscles help: the sternocleidomastoid, scalenes and pectoralis minor assist inspiration, and the internal intercostals and abdominal muscles make expiration active and forceful.

Breathing is controlled by the respiratory centre in the medulla. During exercise it responds to:

  • Chemoreceptors that detect rising carbon dioxide and falling pH (the main driver of increased breathing).
  • Proprioceptors in muscles and joints that signal that movement has started.
  • Higher brain centres that cause an anticipatory increase before exercise begins.

Gas exchange and gas transport

Gases move by diffusion, from an area of higher partial pressure to an area of lower partial pressure. In the lungs, oxygen diffuses from the alveoli into the blood and carbon dioxide diffuses the other way. In the muscles the process reverses: oxygen leaves the blood and carbon dioxide enters it. The alveoli and capillaries have walls only one cell thick and a huge surface area, so exchange is fast. During exercise the gradients at the muscle become steeper because the muscle uses oxygen faster and produces more carbon dioxide, so more gas is exchanged.

Almost all oxygen (about 98%) is carried bound to haemoglobin inside red blood cells, which is why having enough haemoglobin matters for endurance performance. A small amount dissolves in plasma. Most carbon dioxide (about 70%) is carried as bicarbonate in the plasma, with the rest bound to haemoglobin or dissolved in plasma.

What changes the numbers?

The values above are typical, not fixed. Each of the factors below shifts heart rate, stroke volume, cardiac output, blood pressure and ventilation.

FactorEffect
AgeMaximum heart rate falls with age, and maximum stroke volume and cardiac output decline. Resting blood pressure tends to rise.
SexMales usually have a larger heart and greater blood volume, so a larger stroke volume and higher maximum cardiac output. Females often have a higher heart rate at the same absolute workload.
Body sizeLarger people usually have a larger stroke volume, cardiac output and minute ventilation in absolute terms. Smaller people may need a higher heart rate for the same output.
FitnessFitter people have a lower resting and sub-maximal heart rate, a larger stroke volume and a higher maximum cardiac output and minute ventilation.
Type of activityResistance exercise causes larger blood pressure spikes than dynamic exercise. Swimming (horizontal) gives a larger stroke volume and lower heart rate than running (upright), because venous return is easier. Exercise using a small muscle mass (arms) produces a lower cardiac output than exercise using the legs.
IntensityHeart rate, cardiac output, systolic blood pressure, blood redistribution and ventilation all increase as intensity increases.

Putting it together: a 1500 m race

On the start line: anticipation raises heart rate and breathing before the gun fires. During the race: heart rate climbs towards its maximum, stroke volume rises then levels off, and cardiac output increases several-fold. Systolic pressure rises, blood is diverted from the gut and kidneys to the leg muscles, and minute ventilation increases roughly twenty-fold. Blood also carries heat from the legs to the skin. After the race: these variables fall back towards resting values over several minutes.

Exam Tips

🎯
Use the precise termWrite "cardiac output", not "the amount of blood pumped", and "minute ventilation", not "breathing more". Always include the equation (Q = HR × SV, VE = TV × RR) when it helps.
🧮
Show your working and unitsFor calculations, write the equation, substitute the numbers and give the units. Stroke volume is usually in mL, but cardiac output is in L per minute, so convert (1000 mL = 1 L).
📈
Quote the data when you describe a graphFor "describe" or "interpret" questions on data, give the shape and figures from the graph: "Heart rate rose from 70 to 190 bpm, whereas stroke volume levelled off at about 110 mL."
🏃
Name the vessel change"Vasodilation of the arterioles supplying the working muscles" and "vasoconstriction of the arterioles to the gut and kidneys" score. "More blood goes to the muscles" doesn't explain anything. Check the IB command terms.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't say stroke volume keeps rising all the way to exhaustion. In most people it levels off at moderate to high intensity, so further increases in cardiac output come mainly from heart rate. Also remember that muscles get more blood from both a higher cardiac output and redistribution.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1Define cardiac output.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • The volume of blood pumped by the heart (by each ventricle) per minute. Accept: heart rate × stroke volume.
2Distinguish between arteries and veins.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Arteries carry blood away from the heart at high pressure, whereas veins return blood to the heart at low pressure.
  • Arteries have thick, elastic, muscular walls, whereas veins have thinner walls and contain valves to prevent backflow.
3Calculate the cardiac output, in L per minute, of an endurance cyclist with a heart rate of 180 beats per minute and a stroke volume of 190 mL.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume.
  • 180 × 190 = 34 200 mL per minute = 34.2 L per minute (units required for the mark).
4Describe how breathing changes between rest and maximal exercise in a distance runner.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Tidal volume increases, for example from about 0.5 L to 2.5 L or more.
  • Respiration rate increases, for example from about 12 to 40–50 breaths per minute.
  • Because minute ventilation = tidal volume × respiration rate, minute ventilation increases greatly, for example from about 6 L per minute to over 100 L per minute.
5Explain how blood flow is redistributed during exercise.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The sympathetic nervous system causes vasoconstriction of the arterioles to the digestive system and kidneys, reducing blood flow to them.
  • In the working muscles, increased carbon dioxide, hydrogen ions and heat cause vasodilation of the arterioles (and more open capillaries).
  • As a result, the muscles receive a much larger share of cardiac output, about 80% in heavy exercise compared with about 20% at rest.
  • This delivers more oxygen and nutrients to the muscles and removes more carbon dioxide, waste and heat, matching the muscles' increased demand.
