📖 IB Command Terms

What Is the Question Asking You to Do?

Every IB question contains a command term. It tells you what kind of answer to write and how much detail it needs. This guide explains each term, what the examiner is looking for, and how to build an answer that earns the marks.

1. How to use this guide

Before you write a word, do three things with every question:

  1. Find the command term. It is usually the first word of the question (for example "Explain", "Outline", "Evaluate").
  2. Check the depth it asks for. The same content can be asked at any level. "State" wants one fact; "Evaluate" wants a judgement. The mark allocation in brackets, such as [3], tells you how much to write.
  3. Plan the shape of the answer. Decide how many points you need and in what order, then write. This takes less time than rewriting.
Command terms grouped by the depth of answer they require. Higher groups need a longer, more connected response.
The rule that covers most questionsMatch the length and structure of your answer to the mark allocation. One mark usually means one precise point. Four marks usually means four developed points, or a chain of reasoning with a conclusion.

2. Rules that apply to every answer

  • Use the sporting context. If the question names a sport, athlete or situation, refer to it in your answer. Generic answers lose marks.
  • Use precise scientific terms. Write "ground reaction force", "type IIx fibre" or "knowledge of results", not "the push from the floor" or "the fast muscles".
  • Include units and values. Give numbers with their units, and read values from graphs carefully (state the axis and the reading).
  • Answer the question that is asked. If the question asks for the effect on performance, do not spend time on the mechanism unless "explain" is also asked.
  • Do not pad. Extra sentences that repeat a point, or add an unrelated fact, do not earn marks and can contradict what you have already written.
  • Check the time. A two-mark question should take about two minutes. Move on and come back if you are stuck.

4. The terms, one by one

Each entry gives the IB definition, what the examiner is looking for, a step-by-step method, the length to aim for, a worked example and a model answer. The model answers are examples of the standard expected, not the only correct wording. The length guides are typical allocations; check the number of marks on your paper.

Define

IB definition: Give the precise meaning of a word, phrase, concept or physical quantity.
What the examiner wants

One accurate, precise sentence. Use the exact scientific wording, not a loose description.

How to build the answer
  1. Write the term, then give its exact meaning in one sentence.
  2. Include units if the term is a quantity.
  3. Do not add examples unless asked; they earn no marks here and use time.
Length guide

1 mark: one precise sentence.

Example question: Define motor unit. [1]
Model answerA motor neuron and all the muscle fibres that it supplies (innervates).
Watch outA vague definition ("the unit that moves the muscle") will not earn the mark. Use the precise term.

State

IB definition: Give a specific name, value or other brief answer without explanation or calculation.
What the examiner wants

The fact, name, value or unit, and nothing else.

How to build the answer
  1. Give the answer first and stop.
  2. No reasons, no working, no extra sentences.
  3. If a value is requested, include its unit.
Length guide

1 mark: a short phrase, name or value.

Example question: State the name of the neurotransmitter released at the neuromuscular junction. [1]
Model answerAcetylcholine.
Watch outAdding an explanation does not earn extra marks, and it can waste time. A contradictory extra statement can lose the mark.

Identify

IB definition: Provide an answer from a number of possibilities. Recognise and state briefly a distinguishing fact or feature.
What the examiner wants

Pick out the correct answer or feature from what is given, briefly.

How to build the answer
  1. Read every option or feature before you decide.
  2. State your choice clearly and briefly.
  3. In a data question, point to the specific value, line or label concerned.
Length guide

1 mark per item: a brief answer, often a single word or phrase.

Example question: Identify the type of muscle fibre with the greatest resistance to fatigue. [1]
Model answerType I (slow oxidative) fibres.
Watch outDo not confuse identify with describe. "Identify" wants the name or feature; it does not want a paragraph.

Outline

IB definition: Give a brief account or summary.
What the examiner wants

The main points only, in order, without detail or reasons.

How to build the answer
  1. List the key points as short, linked statements.
  2. Keep each point to one clear idea.
  3. Use the correct terms; you do not need to explain them.
Length guide

2 marks: two or three brief points. 3 marks: three points or a short summary sentence per step.

Example question: Outline the role of calcium ions in muscle contraction. [2]
Model answerCalcium is released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. It binds to troponin, which moves tropomyosin and exposes the binding sites on actin.
Watch outOutline is not the same as state. A single fact is usually too little; a full paragraph is usually too much.

Describe

IB definition: Give a detailed account or picture of a situation, event, pattern or process.
What the examiner wants

The "what" and "how it looks": the features, sequence or pattern, with detail. Reasons are not needed, but a pattern in data must be quantified.

How to build the answer
  1. Work through the process or pattern in order.
  2. Use precise terms and values where they are given.
  3. For graphs or data, state the trend, its size, and the range or period it covers.
Length guide

2–4 marks: several developed points, often in sequence.

Example question: Describe the change in heart rate during the first minute of exercise shown in the graph. [2] (illustrative graph)
Model answerHeart rate rises sharply from about 70 to about 120 beats per minute in the first 30 seconds, then rises more slowly and levels off at about 140 bpm by the end of the minute.
Watch outDescribe does not ask why. Keep reasons out unless the question also says "explain".

Distinguish

IB definition: Make clear the differences between two or more concepts or items.
What the examiner wants

Clear statements of how the items differ. Each difference should be stated, not just implied.

How to build the answer
  1. Name both items at the start of each point.
  2. Write one difference per point, using a contrast word such as "whereas" or "unlike".
  3. Make sure each difference is about the same feature (for example both about speed of contraction).
Length guide

2 marks: two clear differences. 3 marks: three clear differences.

Example question: Distinguish between type I and type IIx muscle fibres. [2]
Model answerType I fibres contract slowly, whereas type IIx fibres contract rapidly. Type I fibres have high resistance to fatigue, unlike type IIx fibres, which fatigue quickly.
Watch outA list of features for each item separately is not a distinction. Compare the same feature side by side.

Explain

IB definition: Give a detailed account including reasons or causes.
What the examiner wants

The "why" and "how": a chain of reasoning that links cause to effect, using the correct scientific principle.

How to build the answer
  1. Start with the key fact or principle.
  2. Link each step to the next with "because", "so" or "this leads to".
  3. End with the effect on the performance or outcome asked about.
  4. Relate the chain to the sporting context in the question.
Length guide

2 marks: a cause and an effect. 3–4 marks: a full causal chain, usually three or four linked steps.

Example question: Explain why bending the knees on landing reduces the risk of injury. [3]
Model answerBending the knees increases the time over which the athlete slows down. Because impulse equals force multiplied by time, the same change in momentum is achieved with a smaller average force. A lower peak force reduces the stress on bones, joints and ligaments, so the risk of injury falls.
Watch outListing reasons without connecting them is a common error. Show the link between each step.

Calculate

IB definition: Obtain a numerical answer showing the relevant stages in the working.
What the examiner wants

The correct number with its unit, and the working shown step by step.

How to build the answer
  1. Write the equation you are using.
  2. Substitute the values with their units.
  3. Show the intermediate steps, then state the answer with units and to a sensible number of significant figures.
Length guide

2–3 marks: method marks for the equation and substitution, plus the final answer with units.

Example question: Calculate the momentum of a 90 kg player running at 6 m/s. [2]
Model answerMomentum = mass × velocity = 90 kg × 6 m/s = 540 kg m/s
Watch outMethod marks are often given for the right equation even if the final answer is wrong, so always show the working.

Compare

IB definition: Give an account of the similarities and differences between two or more items or situations, referring to both (all) of them throughout.
What the examiner wants

Points about both items on each point, covering similarities and differences in the same structure.

How to build the answer
  1. Choose a clear set of points to compare, such as speed, fatigue resistance and energy system.
  2. For each point, say what is true of item A and of item B in the same sentence.
  3. Use "both", "similarly" for similarities; "whereas" or "in contrast" for differences.
Length guide

3–4 marks: three or four comparison points, each referring to both items.

Example question: Compare the energy systems used during a 100 m sprint and a 10 km run. [3]
Model answerBoth systems supply energy for exercise, and both use glucose. The sprint relies mainly on the phosphagen and anaerobic glycolysis systems, whereas the 10 km run relies mainly on the aerobic system. Sprinting produces energy faster but for a shorter time, while the aerobic system produces energy more slowly but for much longer.
Watch outWriting about one item, then the other, without referring back, is weak. Refer to both items on every point.

Compare and contrast

IB definition: Give an account of the similarities and differences between two or more items or situations, referring to both (all) of them throughout.
What the examiner wants

Both sides of the comparison, clearly marked. Make sure you cover both similarities and differences.

How to build the answer
  1. Split your points into similarities first, then differences.
  2. Use the same structure as compare, but make sure every point has a similarity or a difference.
Length guide

As for compare, with at least one similarity and one difference in each part.

Example question: Compare and contrast the roles of agonist and antagonist muscles. [3]
Model answerBoth are skeletal muscles that act across the same joint. However, the agonist shortens to produce the movement, whereas the antagonist lengthens and relaxes to allow it.
Watch outIf the question says "compare and contrast", answering only with similarities or only with differences loses marks.

Analyse

IB definition: Break down in order to bring out the essential elements or structure.
What the examiner wants

The parts of the information, the relationships between them, and what they show. For data, the trends, comparisons and values that matter.

How to build the answer
  1. Identify the key elements or variables in the information.
  2. Describe the pattern or relationship, with values.
  3. Interpret what the pattern shows, using the correct scientific terms.
  4. Avoid restating the data without comment.
Length guide

3–5 marks: several linked observations, each with values and an interpretation.

Example question: Analyse the data in the table on the effect of training on VO2 max. [4] (illustrative data)
Model answerVO2 max increases in every training group, with the largest rise (from 45 to 52 ml/kg/min) in the high-intensity group. The control group changes by less than 1 ml/kg/min. This suggests that the training, rather than normal variation, caused the increase, and that higher intensity produced a larger adaptation.
Watch outAnalyse is different from describe. An analysis explains what the pattern shows and how the parts relate, not only what the numbers are.

Suggest

IB definition: Propose a hypothesis or other possible answer. There may not be one correct answer.
What the examiner wants

A reasonable, justified proposal that uses the scientific context. Several answers may be accepted.

How to build the answer
  1. Propose a possible explanation or action.
  2. Link it to the sport, the data or the theory.
  3. Say briefly why it is plausible.
Length guide

1–2 marks: a sensible suggestion with a reason.

Example question: Suggest one reason why the athlete's heart rate was higher than expected at the same workload. [1]
Model answerThe athlete may have been dehydrated, which reduces blood volume and raises heart rate for the same workload.
Watch outDo not treat suggest as "state the right answer". A plausible, well-justified idea scores; a guess with no link to the science does not.

Deduce

IB definition: Reach a conclusion from the information given.
What the examiner wants

A logical conclusion that follows directly from the data or statement given.

How to build the answer
  1. Start from the information in the question or the data.
  2. Apply a relevant principle or rule.
  3. State the conclusion clearly.
Length guide

1–2 marks: the conclusion with a short justification.

Example question: Deduce the direction of the frictional force acting on a sprinter's foot during the push-off. [1]
Model answerBackwards, opposite to the direction of the foot's push on the track, because friction opposes the motion.
Watch outDeduce means the answer should follow logically. A conclusion that does not come from the given information will not score.

Justify

IB definition: Give valid reasons or evidence to support an answer or conclusion.
What the examiner wants

A clear decision or conclusion, backed by reasons and evidence from the scientific context.

How to build the answer
  1. State your answer or conclusion first.
  2. Give two or three reasons or pieces of evidence.
  3. Use values or science principles as evidence.
  4. Link the evidence back to the decision.
Length guide

3–4 marks: a conclusion plus two or three supported reasons.

Example question: Justify the use of a force plate, rather than video analysis, to measure the take-off of a long jump. [3]
Model answerA force plate is the better choice because it measures the ground reaction force directly and allows the impulse and take-off velocity to be calculated. Video analysis can estimate the movement, but it cannot measure the force. Force plates also give precise timing of the take-off, which is needed to calculate velocity accurately.
Watch outDo not just state the conclusion. Justify requires reasons that support it.

Evaluate

IB definition: Make an appraisal by weighing up the strengths and limitations.
What the examiner wants

A judgement, reached by weighing strengths against limitations, usually ending with a conclusion.

How to build the answer
  1. Give at least one strength and one limitation, each explained.
  2. Weigh them up: which matters more, and why?
  3. End with a clear judgement or conclusion.
Length guide

4–6 marks: strengths and limitations, each developed, plus a judgement.

Example question: Evaluate the use of wearable sensors to monitor training load. [4]
Model answerStrength: sensors give objective, continuous data on distance, speed and acceleration in real training, so load can be tracked over weeks. Limitation: accuracy varies between devices and the data may be hard to interpret without expertise. On balance, sensors are valuable for tracking trends, but they should support coaching judgement rather than replace it.
Watch outListing strengths and limitations without a judgement will not reach the top band. Always end with a conclusion.

Discuss

IB definition: Offer a considered and balanced review that includes a range of arguments, factors or hypotheses. Opinions or conclusions should be presented clearly and supported by appropriate evidence.
What the examiner wants

Several viewpoints or factors, each supported by evidence, weighed against each other, with a conclusion.

How to build the answer
  1. Present one argument or factor, then the evidence for it.
  2. Present a counter-argument or limitation, with its evidence.
  3. Weigh them up and reach a clear conclusion.
Length guide

4–6 marks: a balanced review of at least two sides, with evidence and a conclusion.

Example question: Discuss whether intrinsic motivation is more important than extrinsic motivation for long-term participation in sport. [6]
Model answerIntrinsic motivation supports enjoyment and self-determined effort, which predicts long-term participation (Self-determination Theory). However, extrinsic rewards such as selection or sponsorship keep some athletes involved, and they can be more important at early stages or for athletes with limited intrinsic interest. Overall, intrinsic motivation is more reliably linked to long-term participation, although the best approach combines both.
Watch outBalanced does not mean giving every point equal weight. Make a clear judgement at the end.

To what extent

IB definition: Consider the arguments for and against the claim, and reach a judgement.
What the examiner wants

A judgement about how far a claim is true, showing both sides of the argument.

How to build the answer
  1. Give the strongest argument for the claim.
  2. Give the strongest argument against it.
  3. State how far you think the claim is true, and why.
Length guide

As for discuss: both sides, evidence, and a clear judgement.

Example question: To what extent is training intensity the most important factor in improving VO2 max? [6]
Model answerIntensity is a major driver: high-intensity training produces larger increases in VO2 max than low-intensity training for the same time. However, duration, frequency and the athlete's starting fitness also affect the outcome, and the effect of intensity is limited for well-trained athletes. Intensity is therefore one of the most important factors, but not the only one.
Watch outDo not answer with "yes" or "no" alone. Show the arguments and reach a graded judgement.

Comment

IB definition: Give a judgement based on a given statement or result.
What the examiner wants

A judgement about the statement or result, with a reason.

How to build the answer
  1. State whether you agree, disagree or see it as partly true.
  2. Give a reason based on the science or the data.
  3. Keep the judgement tied to the statement.
Length guide

2–3 marks: a judgement and one or two supported reasons.

Example question: Comment on the statement: "Stretching before exercise prevents all injuries." [2]
Model answerThis is not supported by the evidence. Stretching has not been shown to prevent all injuries, although a proper warm-up may reduce some types of injury. The statement overgeneralises.
Watch outA judgement without a reason is not enough. Support the judgement with the science.

Draw, label and annotate

IB definition: Draw: represent by means of a labelled, accurate diagram or graph. Label: add labels to a diagram. Annotate: add brief notes to a diagram.
What the examiner wants

An accurate, neat diagram, with labels and short notes where asked.

How to build the answer
  1. Draw with a sharp pencil and use a ruler for straight lines.
  2. Make the diagram large enough to show detail.
  3. Add labels to each named part, and notes if asked to annotate.
  4. Give a title and any units for a graph.
Length guide

Marks are given for accuracy, labels and any required annotations.

Example question: Draw and label a diagram of a sarcomere in the relaxed state. [3]
Model answerA labelled sketch showing Z-discs at each end, thin (actin) filaments, thick (myosin) filaments, the H zone and the A band, with the I band marked.
Watch outA diagram with no labels, or with labels that do not match the diagram, will lose marks even if it is accurate.

5. Before you finish any answer

  • Have I answered with the command term's depth (not more, not less)?
  • Have I referred to the sport or situation in the question?
  • Have I included units, values and correct scientific terms?
  • If I was asked to evaluate, discuss or justify, did I reach a clear conclusion?
  • Did I answer every part of the question, including every named item in "compare" or "distinguish"?
