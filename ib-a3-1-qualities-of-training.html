A.3.1 Theme A · Response SL & HL

Qualities of Training

The principles that make a training programme safe and effective, and how coaches design, measure and periodise training so the body adapts without breaking down.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Training works by applying a stress the body is not used to, then allowing time to recover and adapt. A good programme gets the balance right: enough stress to cause improvement, enough rest to recover, and a plan that matches the athlete and the sport. Coaches use a set of training principles (specificity, progressive overload, recovery, variety, reversibility and periodisation) and manipulate the frequency, intensity and duration of training. They also measure a baseline so that progress can be tracked, and they adjust the plan to suit factors such as fitness level, age and sex.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Define and apply specificity, progressive overload, recovery, variety, reversibility and periodisation.
  • Explain how frequency, intensity and duration can be changed to overload training safely.
  • Explain why baseline values and progress measurement matter.
  • Describe the structure of periodised training: macrocycles, mesocycles and microcycles.
  • Explain how factors such as current fitness, age and sex affect programme design.
  • Apply these ideas to design a programme for a named athlete or sport.

Key Terms

SpecificityTraining adaptations match the demands of the training: the sport, muscles, movements and energy systems used.
Progressive overloadGradually increasing the training stress so the body keeps adapting.
ReversibilityFitness gains are lost when training stops or is reduced.
PeriodisationPlanned variation of training across cycles to balance load, recovery and peak performance.
TaperA planned reduction in training volume before a key event, keeping intensity high.
Baseline valueA starting measurement, used to set goals and judge progress.

Deep Dive

The training principles

The principles work together. A programme that applies only one of them (for example, overload with no recovery) is likely to fail.

PrincipleWhat it meansSporting example
SpecificityAdaptations match what is trained: the energy system, muscle groups, movement pattern and goal. Not every exercise must copy the sport, as supporting exercises can build strength or stability.A marathon runner does long, steady runs; a sprinter does short, maximal sprints and power work.
Progressive overloadTraining stress is increased gradually, by raising frequency, intensity or duration, so the body must keep adapting. Too fast risks injury; too slow causes a plateau.A runner adds a little distance or pace each week; a lifter adds a small amount of weight.
RecoveryAdaptation happens during rest, not during the session. Sleep, nutrition and rest days allow repair and rebuilding. Too little leads to fatigue, poor performance and overtraining.Easy days and rest days between hard sessions; sleep and food after training.
VarietyChanging exercises or sessions in a planned way to keep the stimulus fresh, reduce overuse injury and maintain motivation, while still supporting the same goal. Random change is not variety.A cyclist adds hill sessions and strength work to long rides.
Reversibility"Use it or lose it": gains fall when training stops or is cut back, for example during injury or the off-season.A footballer's aerobic fitness drops after several weeks of no training.
PeriodisationPlanned variation of training load across cycles, so that stress, recovery and peak performance are balanced.A swimmer builds a base in the preparation phase and peaks for the national championships.

Overload, recovery and adaptation

When training stress is applied, performance drops temporarily because of fatigue. During recovery the body repairs and rebuilds, and adapts to be slightly better able to cope with the same stress. Frequency, intensity and duration

To overload safely, a coach changes the training variables, ideally one at a time:

  • Frequency: how often the athlete trains, for example sessions per week.
  • Intensity: how hard the work is. It can be measured with heart rate (as a percentage of maximum heart rate), rating of perceived exertion (RPE), percentage of one-repetition maximum (1RM) for strength work, or running or cycling speed or power.
  • Duration: how long each session lasts or, for strength training, how much work is done (volume = sets × repetitions × load).

Changing two at once, such as longer sessions at a higher intensity, creates a much bigger jump in load and raises the risk of injury and overtraining. A common rule of thumb is to increase weekly load by no more than about 10%, but this is only a guide and should be adjusted for the athlete. Many programmes also include easier recovery weeks.

Baseline values and measuring progress

A baseline value is a starting measurement, such as a fitness test result, resting heart rate or body mass. It allows a coach to choose a safe starting load, set realistic goals and later judge whether the programme is working. Good practice includes:

  • Using valid, reliable tests that match the goal (for example a 1RM test for strength or a timed run for aerobic fitness), carried out under the same conditions each time.
  • Retesting often enough to guide decisions, but not so often that testing replaces training or causes fatigue.
  • Using the results to change the plan: progress the load, vary the sessions, or ease off if the athlete is not coping.

Designing a programme for an individual

Programme design is a cycle: assess, plan, train, monitor and adjust.

The training programme design cycle. The factors at the bottom shape every stage of the plan.
  • Current fitness level: beginners need a lower starting load and slower progression; trained athletes can tolerate higher intensity and more complex training.
  • Age: affects recovery time, tolerance of load and injury risk. Young athletes are still growing and need careful loading, and older athletes usually need more recovery.
  • Sex and reproductive status: there are physiological differences between males and females (for example in body composition, hormones and energy needs), and some female athletes notice changes in energy or recovery across the menstrual cycle, or need adjusted training during pregnancy and after childbirth. Differences between individuals are often bigger than differences between the sexes, so plans should be individual and flexible.
  • Training history, health and injuries: past injuries and medical conditions limit what is safe.
  • Goals, sport and time available: the demands of the sport and the time the athlete has decide the content (specificity).

Periodisation

Periodisation divides training into planned cycles so that load, recovery and peak performance are balanced across the year.

  • Macrocycle: the long-term plan, such as a season or year, built around the main competition.
  • Mesocycle: a block of several weeks within the macrocycle, with a particular focus (for example aerobic base, strength or speed).
  • Microcycle: typically one week of sessions, mixing hard, moderate and easy days.

The macrocycle usually has three phases. In the preparation phase, training is first general (building a base with high volume at lower intensity) and then more specific to the sport. In the competition phase, intensity is high and volume is lower, with a taper before the key event to remove fatigue while keeping fitness. The transition phase gives active rest so the athlete can recover physically and mentally before the next cycle.

Exam Tips

🎯
Define, then apply to the sport namedGive a short definition of the principle, then show it in action for the athlete in the question: "Specificity: a marathon runner mainly trains aerobically, with long runs."
🔧
Name the variable you changeFor "explain how to apply overload", say which of frequency, intensity or duration changes, and by how much, for example "increase weekly distance by about 10%".
🗓️
Name the cycle and the phaseFor periodisation answers, say whether you mean the macrocycle, mesocycle or microcycle, and which phase (preparation, competition or transition). Check the IB command terms.
📏
Link measurement to decisionsBaseline values are used to set a safe starting load and to show progress. Say what the coach does with the result.
⚠️
Common mistakeProgressive overload does not mean training harder every session. It means planned, gradual increases with recovery built in. Increasing too fast, or without rest, causes overtraining and injury.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1Define specificity.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • The principle that training adaptations are specific to the type of training done, so training should match the demands of the sport (for example its energy systems, muscles and movement patterns).
2Outline two ways a coach could apply progressive overload for an endurance runner.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Gradually increase the weekly distance (duration) of the runs.
  • Gradually increase intensity, for example faster pace or interval sessions.
  • Add an extra training session each week (frequency), or reduce recovery time between intervals.
  • Any two, with the increase described as gradual.
3Describe the structure of a periodised training plan using the terms macrocycle, mesocycle and microcycle.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The macrocycle is the long-term plan, such as a whole season or year, built around the main competition.
  • A mesocycle is a block of several weeks within the macrocycle, with a particular training focus (for example aerobic base or strength).
  • A microcycle is a short cycle, typically one week, made up of individual sessions with hard, moderate and easy days.
4Explain why a coach must consider an athlete's current fitness level when designing a training programme.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Baseline testing shows the athlete's starting level, so that a suitable starting load can be chosen.
  • If the load is too high, the athlete risks injury, excessive fatigue or overtraining; if too low, there is no overload and so no adaptation.
  • The baseline allows progress to be measured later and the programme adjusted accordingly.
5Explain how the principles of specificity and progressive overload could be applied in a 12-week programme for a recreational 5 km runner.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Specificity: most sessions are running-based, using aerobic runs plus some interval sessions near race pace, so the training matches the energy systems and movements of a 5 km race.
  • Progressive overload: weekly distance and/or intensity is increased gradually (for example by about 10% a week) rather than suddenly.
  • Recovery weeks or rest days are included so the body can adapt and the risk of injury is reduced.
  • Progress is measured, for example with a baseline and repeat 5 km time trial, and the plan is adjusted using the results.
