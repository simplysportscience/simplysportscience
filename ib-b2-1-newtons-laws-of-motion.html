B.2.1 Theme B · Forces, Motion & Movement SL & HL

Newton's Laws of Motion

How forces cause and change movement: the three laws, linear and angular motion, momentum and impulse, and at higher level collisions, friction, work, energy and power.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Every movement in sport follows Newton's three laws. They explain why an athlete needs a force to start moving, how force and mass decide acceleration, and how pushing on the ground moves the body. They apply to linear motion (in a straight line) and angular motion (rotation about an axis). Momentum and impulse link force, time and change of motion, which explains everything from landing safely to hitting a ball further. At higher level, you also study collisions, friction, and work, energy and power.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • State Newton's three laws and apply them to sport in both linear and angular contexts.
  • Distinguish between distance, displacement, speed, velocity and acceleration, and interpret a velocity–time graph.
  • Explain momentum and impulse, and use them to explain performance and injury risk.
  • Explain angular motion, moment of inertia, angular momentum and the effect of an eccentric force.
  • HL: Apply conservation of momentum to collisions.
  • HL: Explain friction using the coefficient of friction, and calculate work, energy and power.

Key Terms

MomentumThe quantity of motion of an object: mass × velocity (kg m/s).
ImpulseForce × time of application; it equals the change in momentum.
Ground reaction force (GRF)The force the ground exerts back on a person who pushes on it.
Moment of inertiaA body's resistance to a change in rotation; it depends on mass and how far it is from the axis.
Angular momentumThe quantity of rotation: moment of inertia × angular velocity.
Eccentric forceA force whose line of action does not pass through the centre of mass, causing rotation.

Deep Dive

Newton's three laws

A force is a push or pull, measured in newtons (N). The net force is the total effect of all the forces on an object. The three laws explain how net force affects motion:

Newton's three laws with a sporting illustration of each. In the third law, the ground reaction force (GRF) is what drives a runner forward.
  • First law (inertia): an object stays at rest, or keeps moving at constant velocity, unless a net external force acts on it. Inertia (resistance to a change in motion) increases with mass, so a heavy rugby forward is harder to stop or start than a light winger.
  • Second law: the acceleration of an object is proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to its mass: F = m × a (force in N, mass in kg, acceleration in m/s²). A sprinter who pushes harder against the blocks accelerates more.
  • Third law: when one object exerts a force on another, the second exerts an equal and opposite force on the first. The two forces act on different objects. A runner pushes backwards and down on the track, and the ground reaction force pushes the runner forwards and up. A swimmer pushing back on the water is pushed forward.

A scalar has size only; a vector has size and direction. A runner who completes one 400 m lap in 50 s has covered a distance of 400 m, but their displacement is 0 m (they finish where they started), so their average speed is 8 m/s and their average velocity is 0 m/s.

Momentum and impulse

Momentum is mass × velocity (p = mv, in kg m/s). To change an object's momentum, a force must act for some time. The product is the impulse:

Impulse = force × time = change in momentum

This explains many sporting techniques. A follow-through in golf, tennis or throwing lengthens the time the force is applied to the ball, which increases the impulse and so the speed of the ball. In the opposite case, when landing, the athlete must lose a fixed amount of momentum. Bending the knees makes the stopping time longer, so the peak force on the bones and joints is lower.

Two landings with the same change in momentum (equal areas). The longer contact time of a bent-knee landing means a much lower peak force. Values are illustrative.

Angular motion

Angular motion is rotation about an axis, such as a somersault (axis through the hips) or a golf swing. The key quantities are angular displacement (the angle turned, in degrees or radians), angular velocity (ω, the angle turned per second) and angular acceleration. Newton's laws have angular versions: a body keeps rotating at constant angular velocity unless an external torque acts, torque produces angular acceleration, and torques come in equal and opposite pairs.

  • Eccentric force: a force whose line of action passes through the centre of mass (a centric force) produces movement without rotation. An eccentric force, applied off-centre, creates torque and so rotation, as well as movement. A footballer who strikes the ball off-centre makes it spin.
  • Moment of inertia (I): resistance to changes in rotation. It depends on the mass and on how far the mass is from the axis: the further away, the greater the moment of inertia.
  • Angular momentum (L = I × ω): the quantity of rotation. In the air, when no external torque acts (ignoring air resistance), angular momentum is conserved.
Left: an off-centre force produces spin. Centre and right: the same angular momentum can be a slow spin with a large moment of inertia, or a fast spin with a small one.

Because angular momentum is conserved in flight, a diver or gymnast can control rotation by changing shape. Tucking pulls mass towards the axis, reducing the moment of inertia, so angular velocity increases and the somersault speeds up. Extending the body before entry or landing increases the moment of inertia and slows the rotation. The angular momentum itself is created at take-off by an eccentric force pushing off the board or ground.

Collisions and conservation of momentum HL

In a collision with no external force, the total momentum before equals the total momentum after (conservation of momentum): m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁′ + m₂v₂′. In an elastic collision, kinetic energy is also conserved (a bouncing superball); in an inelastic collision some is lost as heat and sound, and the objects may move together (a rugby tackle).

Worked example: a 90 kg runner moving at 6 m/s tackles a stationary 100 kg player, and they move together. Momentum before = 90 × 6 = 540 kg m/s. Momentum after = 190 × v. So v = 540 ÷ 190 = 2.8 m/s.

Friction HL

Friction is a contact force parallel to the surface that opposes motion or attempted motion between two surfaces. Its maximum size depends on the coefficient of friction (μ), which depends on the two surfaces, and on the normal reaction force (N) pressing them together:

Friction = μ × N

For example, if μ = 0.8 and N = 700 N, the maximum friction is 0.8 × 700 = 560 N. Static friction (before sliding) is usually greater than kinetic friction (while sliding). Friction is essential for running, as without it the foot would slip and no forward push could be obtained. Athletes increase friction with studs, spikes and chalk, and reduce it where speed matters, as with skates on ice or waxed skis. A wet surface lowers μ and increases slipping and injury risk.

Exam Tips

🎯
Name the law and apply itSay which law, state it briefly, then apply it to the sport: "Third law: the runner pushes back on the ground and the ground reaction force pushes the runner forward."
🔗
Use impulse for "time" questionsFor landing, follow-through or catching, say: impulse = force × time. A longer time reduces force for the same change in momentum.
🌀
Link angular momentum to shapeFor somersaults, say that angular momentum is conserved, so a smaller moment of inertia (tuck) gives a larger angular velocity. Check the IB command terms.
🧮
Show equations and unitsWrite the equation, substitute, then give the answer with units (N, kg m/s, m/s², J, W). Don't mix up scalars and vectors.
⚠️
Common mistakeAction and reaction forces act on different objects, so they don't cancel each other out. Also, don't write that a body "has" a force: say that a force acts on it.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1State Newton's first law of motion.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • An object remains at rest, or continues to move at constant velocity, unless acted on by a net (unbalanced) external force.
2State Newton's third law and give a sporting example.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • For every action force there is an equal and opposite reaction force.
  • For example, a swimmer pushes back on the water and is pushed forward; or a runner pushes back and down on the track and the ground reaction force pushes the runner forward and up.
3(HL) A 90 kg rugby player running at 6 m/s tackles a stationary 100 kg player and they move together. Calculate their velocity immediately after the tackle.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Momentum is conserved: momentum before = momentum after.
  • Momentum before = 90 × 6 = 540 kg m/s.
  • 540 = (90 + 100) × v, so v = 2.8 m/s (units required).
4Explain why bending the knees when landing from a jump reduces the risk of injury.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The athlete must lose a fixed amount of momentum on landing, so the impulse is the same.
  • Bending the knees increases the time over which the force acts.
  • Since impulse = force × time, a longer time means a lower average and peak force, so there is less stress on bones and joints.
5Explain how a diver can increase the speed of rotation during a somersault and control the entry into the water.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Angular momentum is created at take-off by an eccentric force from the board, and is conserved in the air because no external torque acts.
  • Tucking moves mass closer to the axis, reducing the moment of inertia.
  • Since angular momentum = moment of inertia × angular velocity, the angular velocity increases, so the diver rotates faster.
  • Extending the body before entry increases the moment of inertia and slows the rotation, allowing a controlled entry.
