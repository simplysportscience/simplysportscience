B.2.2 Theme B · Forces, Motion & Movement SL & HL

Fluid Mechanics

How air and water push back on moving athletes and objects: projectile flight, drag, lift, spin and buoyancy, and how athletes use or avoid them.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Air and water are fluids, and anything moving through them is pushed on by them. For a projectile (a javelin, a ball, or a long jumper in flight) the path depends on release speed, angle and height and on the fluid forces acting on it. At higher level, you study how fluids act on athletes in more detail: drag slows movement, lift acts at right angles to the flow, spin causes the Magnus effect, and buoyancy pushes up on a body in water. Athletes, coaches and equipment designers use these effects to gain an advantage, and governing bodies regulate them.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Describe the factors that affect the flight path of a projectile and the forces that act on it.
  • Explain how release angle, speed and height affect distance in sport.
  • HL: Explain how environmental conditions change the forces acting on athletes and objects.
  • HL: Explain surface, form and wave drag, and how athletes reduce drag.
  • HL: Explain lift, Bernoulli's principle, angle of attack, the Magnus effect and buoyancy.
  • HL: Discuss ethical and regulatory issues in using fluid mechanics to improve performance.

Key Terms

ProjectileAn object in flight that is acted on only by gravity and fluid forces such as air resistance.
DragA resistive force that opposes motion through a fluid.
LiftA force at right angles to the direction of fluid flow, caused by a pressure difference.
Angle of attackThe angle between an object and the direction of the fluid flow.
Magnus effectA force that curves the path of a spinning object moving through a fluid.
BuoyancyThe upward force from a fluid, equal to the weight of the fluid displaced.

Deep Dive

Projectile motion

A projectile is an object moving through the air after release, such as a thrown javelin, a kicked ball or an athlete in the flight phase of a long jump. Once released, the only forces acting on it are its weight (gravity, always downwards) and fluid forces, mainly air resistance (and sometimes lift or a Magnus force). A projectile's initial velocity can be split into a horizontal part (v cos θ) and a vertical part (v sin θ). Without air resistance the horizontal velocity stays constant, while gravity changes the vertical velocity at 9.81 m/s² downwards, which gives the curved, parabola-shaped path.

The factors that determine the flight path and distance are:

  • Release speed: the most important factor. Distance increases with the square of speed, so a small gain in speed gives a big gain in distance.
  • Release angle: if release and landing are at the same height and there is no air resistance, 45° gives the greatest range, and angles that add up to 90° (such as 30° and 60°) give the same range.
  • Release height: releasing from higher up gives a longer flight time and a longer distance.
  • Air resistance, spin and lift: these change the path in real sport.
Paths for five release angles at the same speed, with no air resistance and equal release and landing heights. 45° gives the greatest range, and complementary angles give equal ranges.

In real sport the best angle is usually less than 45°. In the shot put the shot is released well above the ground, and the best angle is about 35–40°. In the long jump the athlete takes off at about 20° because a steeper take-off would cost too much horizontal speed. The javelin is thrown at about 30–35°, partly because it generates lift.

The path of a projectile released from 1.8 m, with and without air resistance (illustrative). Air resistance shortens the flight and makes the descent steeper.

Environmental conditions HL

  • Air density: thinner air at altitude, or hot or humid air, gives less drag, so projectiles and runners meet less resistance. This helped long distances and sprints at the 1968 Mexico City Olympics.
  • Wind: a tailwind reduces the air speed relative to an athlete and lowers drag; a headwind increases it. Wind affects projectiles and cyclists strongly.
  • Water: the density of the water changes buoyancy. Salt water is denser than fresh water, so swimmers float higher in it. Water temperature also affects thickness (viscosity) and comfort.

Drag HL

Drag is a force that opposes motion through a fluid. It increases with the speed (roughly with the square of speed), the frontal area of the body, the shape (streamlined shapes have less drag) and the density of the fluid. Water is much denser than air, so drag in water is far greater than in air. There are three types:

The three types of drag. Athletes reduce drag with smoother surfaces, streamlined shapes and (in water) staying below the surface.
  • Surface drag (skin friction): friction between the fluid and the surface of the body or equipment. Smooth suits, shaved skin and swim caps reduce it.
  • Form drag (pressure drag): caused by the shape and frontal area of the body, which leaves a low-pressure wake behind. A streamlined body position, such as a cyclist in an aerodynamic crouch, reduces it.
  • Wave drag: energy used to make waves at the water surface. It matters for swimmers and rowers, and is reduced by swimming below the surface (as in the underwater phase after a swim start).

Cyclists also reduce drag by drafting behind another rider. Drag is not always unwanted: swimmers use hand shape to create useful drag when pushing water backwards, and parachutes, and skydivers use it to slow down.

Lift, Bernoulli's principle and the Magnus effect HL

Lift is a force at right angles to the direction of the fluid flow. According to Bernoulli's principle, where a fluid moves faster its pressure is lower, so if the flow is faster on one side of an object than the other, the higher pressure on the slower side pushes the object towards the faster side. The angle of attack (the angle between the object and the oncoming flow) controls how much lift and drag there are: a small angle can increase lift, but too large an angle causes the flow to separate and lift to fall. Discus and javelin throwers, ski jumpers and swimmers sculling their hands all use lift.

The Magnus effect is a sideways force on a spinning ball. The spin drags air round with it, so the air is slowed on one side and speeds up on the other, creating a pressure difference. With topspin the ball dips sooner (a topspin forehand in tennis); with backspin the ball floats further (a golf drive); and with sidespin the ball curves sideways (a bending free kick).

Buoyancy is the upward force a fluid exerts on a body in it. It equals the weight of the fluid displaced, so it depends on the density of the fluid and the volume displaced. People with a higher body fat percentage or full lungs float higher, and a wetsuit adds buoyancy, helping a swimmer keep a flatter, less draggy position.

Using fluid mechanics: ethics and regulation HL

Knowing fluid mechanics lets athletes and engineers improve performance through technique (body position, hand shape), equipment (smooth swimsuits, aerodynamic helmets and bikes) and environment (choosing a fast, high-altitude venue). This raises fairness questions. Equipment can be expensive, so wealthy athletes and nations may gain an advantage, and some technology can change results more than athletic skill. Governing bodies therefore set rules: for example, full-body polyurethane swimsuits, which gave large buoyancy and drag benefits, were banned in swimming after the 2008–2009 seasons, and cycling and javelin specifications are tightly controlled. Regulation tries to balance innovation with fair competition.

Exam Tips

🎯
List the forces on a projectileWeight (gravity) and air resistance, plus lift or Magnus force if it spins. Say that weight always acts downwards.
📐
Link angle to the situation45° is the best angle only for equal release and landing heights with no air resistance. In the shot put, long jump and javelin it is lower, so give the reason.
🏊
Name the type of dragFor "reduce drag" questions, say which type (surface, form or wave) and how: for example, "a tucked position reduces frontal area, so reduces form drag". Check the IB command terms.
🎾
Explain Magnus with pressureDescribe which side the air is sped up (lower pressure) and which side it is slowed (higher pressure), and the direction of the resulting force on the ball.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't say heavier objects always fall faster or that 45° is always optimal. Without air resistance, all objects fall with the same acceleration, and optimal angles change with release height, lift and sport.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1State the release angle that gives the greatest range for a projectile released and landing at the same height, ignoring air resistance.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • 45°.
2Outline two forces that act on a projectile in flight.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Weight (gravity), acting downwards.
  • Air resistance (drag), acting against the direction of motion. (Lift or the Magnus force on a spinning projectile is also accepted.)
3Describe three factors that influence the horizontal distance travelled by a projectile.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Release speed: a higher speed gives a greater distance.
  • Release angle: the angle should be close to the optimum for the situation (45° if heights are equal).
  • Release height: a higher release gives a longer flight time and a longer distance. (Air resistance, spin and lift are also accepted.) Any three.
4(HL) Explain how a cyclist can reduce form drag and surface drag.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Adopting a low, tucked (aerodynamic) position reduces the frontal area, which reduces form drag.
  • Wearing smooth, tight clothing and a helmet, or shaving the legs, reduces the friction between the air and the body, which reduces surface drag.
  • Drafting behind another rider reduces the air resistance on the cyclist. Any three.
5(HL) Explain how the Magnus effect causes a ball hit with topspin to dip.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The ball spins forwards (topspin), so the top surface moves against the oncoming air flow.
  • The air above the ball is slowed, giving higher pressure on top; the air below is sped up, giving lower pressure underneath.
  • The pressure difference creates a Magnus force acting downwards (from the high pressure to the low pressure).
  • This force adds to gravity, so the ball dips sooner and a shorter, steeper path results.
