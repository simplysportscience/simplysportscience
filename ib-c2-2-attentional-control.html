C.2.2 Theme C · Motor Learning SL & HL

Attentional Control

How athletes direct, maintain and shift their focus onto the cues that matter, and why pressure, arousal and distractions can disrupt it.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Attentional control is the ability to direct, maintain and shift focus onto the cues that matter most for a skill. Attention can be described along two dimensions: its width (broad or narrow) and its direction (external, on the environment or the outcome of a movement, or internal, on the body or thoughts). Effective skill execution depends on using the right focus at the right moment. Pressure, arousal, fatigue and distractions can narrow or scatter attention, and athletes can use strategies such as cue words and routines to keep their focus where it needs to be.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Distinguish between broad and narrow attentional focus, and between internal and external focus.
  • Give a sporting example of each type of attentional focus.
  • Explain how arousal and pressure can narrow attention and affect performance.
  • Describe strategies that athletes use to control their attentional focus, such as self-talk and routines.
  • Explain how external and internal distractors can impair performance.
  • Explain why an external focus is often more effective than an internal focus for skilled motor performance.

Key Terms

Attentional controlThe ability to direct, maintain and shift focus onto the most relevant cues for successful skill execution.
Broad focusAttention spread across many cues at once, useful for scanning and decision-making.
Narrow focusAttention restricted to one or two key cues, useful for precise execution.
External focusAttention on the environment or the effect of the movement, such as the ball's flight.
Internal focusAttention on the body, thoughts or feelings, such as the position of a joint.
Self-talkUsing spoken or silent cue words or phrases to direct attention, confidence and action.

Deep Dive

Width and direction of attention

Attentional focus has two dimensions, and each combination suits a different demand. Broad focus takes in many cues, which helps with scanning, tactical awareness and decision-making in team sports. Narrow focus picks out one or two cues, which helps precise execution, such as a golf putt or a free throw. External focus is directed at the environment or at the outcome of the movement, while internal focus is directed at the body or at thoughts and feelings.

The four combinations of attentional width and direction, with a sporting example for each. Athletes move between these as the demands of the situation change.

Each type has a use. In a fast team game, a player needs broad external focus to read teammates and opponents before choosing an action. A goalkeeper facing a penalty needs narrow external focus on the ball and the taker's movement. Broad internal focus helps with planning and analysing tactics or previous performance, while narrow internal focus can support breathing control, emotional regulation and mental rehearsal before a skill.

External or internal focus in motor performance

Research on motor learning has found that an external focus on the intended outcome of a movement often leads to better performance and faster learning than an internal focus on body movements. For example, a basketball player focusing on the ball going through the hoop tends to perform better than one focusing on each joint of the shooting action. A plausible explanation is that an internal focus makes the performer consciously control movements that are better organised automatically, whereas an external focus allows movement to self-organise more naturally. The evidence is strongest for relatively simple, well-learned skills, so the effect should be applied with judgement.

Arousal, pressure and attentional narrowing

Arousal (physiological and psychological activation) affects attention. At low arousal, attention may be wide but scattered, and relevant cues can be missed. At an optimal level of arousal, attention is focused on the relevant cues. At high arousal, such as the anxiety of a penalty in a final, attention tends to narrow, so the performer focuses on one thing, often the threat, and misses other relevant cues such as the goalkeeper's movement. This is linked to the arousal and anxiety topic (see C.4.1).

How arousal can affect attention in a penalty kick (illustrative). Attention is widest at low arousal, focused at optimal arousal, and narrowest at high arousal, when relevant cues are missed.

Strategies to control attentional focus

Athletes can learn to direct and control their attention with several strategies:

  • Self-talk: a short cue word or phrase, spoken or silent, that directs attention and supports confidence and action. A cue such as "smooth" can keep a swimmer's attention on rhythm.
  • Pre-performance routines: the same sequence of actions before each skill, which narrows attention to the task and manages arousal.
  • Mental rehearsal: imagining the skill before performing it, which focuses attention on the key cues and the feel of the movement (see C.5.2).
  • Cue focus: choosing a relevant external cue, such as a target, rather than a list of technical points.

Distractors and their effects

Distractors pull attention away from the relevant cues. External distractors include crowd noise, a heckling opponent, glare or a referee's whistle. Internal distractors include worry about the scoreline, thoughts about a previous mistake, fatigue and fear of failure. Distractors can impair performance by occupying attention that the skill needs, and by pushing the performer towards an internal focus at the wrong moment. Attentional control training helps athletes keep focus on relevant cues despite both kinds of distraction.

Internal and external cues for a basketball free throw, the strategies that help maintain focus, and examples of distractors. Strategies work by directing attention, not by ignoring the situation.

Evaluating attentional control

Attentional control can be measured with tests in which athletes respond to relevant and irrelevant cues, and with self-report questionnaires. Both have limits: laboratory tests may not reflect the pressure and noise of competition, and self-reports depend on how athletes perceive their own focus. Attentional strategies also differ between athletes and between skills, so coaches should test strategies in training before using them in competition.

Exam Tips

🎯
Name both dimensionsDescribe attention by its width (broad or narrow) and its direction (internal or external). Give a sporting example of each. Check the IB command terms.
🧠
Link arousal to narrowingFor high-pressure questions, say that arousal can narrow attention onto a threat and cause relevant cues to be missed, then give the example.
🏀
Match focus to the skillUse an external focus for simple, well-learned skills where the outcome is the cue. Use broad focus for scanning in team games.
🔤
Explain strategies with a cueFor strategy questions, give the strategy, the cue or routine, and how it directs attention. For example: "a cue word such as 'smooth' keeps the swimmer's attention on rhythm."
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't say that narrow focus is always better, or that broad focus is always worse. Each suits a different demand. Also, don't confuse internal focus with being "focused": an internal focus can be useful for some tasks, but may hurt skilled performance.
