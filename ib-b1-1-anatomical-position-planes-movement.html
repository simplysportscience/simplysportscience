B.1.1 Theme B · Generating Movement in the Body SL & HL

Anatomical Position, Planes & Movement

The shared language of movement: how to describe where things are on the body, which way joints move, and in which plane and around which axis.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

To analyse movement, everyone needs the same reference system. The anatomical position is the standard starting point, and directional terms such as anterior and distal describe where one body part is relative to another. The skeleton gives the body its framework, and is split into an axial and an appendicular part. Movements at joints happen in planes (sagittal, frontal and transverse) and rotate around axes at right angles to those planes. At higher level, you also study anthropometry: measuring the size and proportions of the body and applying the results in sport and design.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Describe the anatomical position and use directional terms correctly.
  • Outline the axial and appendicular skeleton and the functions of the skeleton.
  • Identify the sagittal, frontal and transverse planes and the axis of rotation for each.
  • Name the movements at the main joints and the plane in which each occurs.
  • HL: Explain how anthropometric measurements are used in sport, health and design.

Key Terms

Anatomical positionStanding upright, facing forward, arms by the sides, palms facing forward, feet together or slightly apart.
Plane of movementAn imaginary flat surface through the body in which a movement takes place.
Axis of rotationThe imaginary line, at right angles to the plane, around which a body part rotates.
FlexionA movement that decreases the angle at a joint.
AbductionMovement of a limb away from the midline of the body.
Anthropometry (HL)The measurement of the size, shape and proportions of the human body.

Deep Dive

Anatomical position and directional terms

In the anatomical position the body stands upright and faces forward, with the arms by the sides, palms facing forward, and the feet together or slightly apart and pointing forward. All movement and location terms are described from this position, even when the performer is moving or upside down. This means the terms always mean the same thing to everyone.

The figure is in anatomical position. The four pairs of directional terms describe where one structure is relative to another.
TermMeaningExample
Superior / inferiorTowards the head / towards the feetThe head is superior to the chest.
Anterior / posteriorTowards the front / towards the backThe quadriceps are on the anterior thigh.
Medial / lateralTowards the midline / away from the midlineThe little toe is lateral to the big toe.
Proximal / distalNearer to / further from the trunk (or the point of attachment)The elbow is proximal to the wrist.
Superficial / deepNearer to / further from the surface of the bodyThe skin is superficial to the muscles.

The skeleton: axial and appendicular

An adult skeleton has 206 bones. It is divided into two parts:

Axial skeleton (80 bones)Appendicular skeleton (126 bones)
PartsSkull, vertebral column, ribs and sternumPectoral (shoulder) girdle, arms and hands, pelvic girdle, legs and feet
Main rolesProtects the brain, spinal cord, heart and lungs; supports the head and trunk; central axis for postureAllows movement of the limbs, for locomotion and manipulation of objects
Sport linkRibcage protects organs in contact sports; the spine supports the trunk in all lifting and throwingLeg bones act as levers in running and jumping; arm bones in throwing and hitting

The skeleton as a whole has five main functions: support (framework for the body), protection of organs, movement (bones act as levers and muscles pull on them), storage of minerals such as calcium and phosphorus, and blood cell production in red bone marrow.

Planes and axes

Movements at joints take place in planes, and the body part rotates around an axis. The axis is always at right angles to the plane of movement.

The three planes through a figure in anatomical position, the three axes of rotation, and a summary of the movements in each plane. The axis is perpendicular to its plane.
  • Sagittal plane: divides the body into left and right. Forward and backward movements such as flexion and extension happen here, around the frontal axis (also called the mediolateral axis).
  • Frontal plane (also called the coronal plane): divides the body into front and back. Sideways movements such as abduction and adduction happen here, around the sagittal axis (also called the anteroposterior axis).
  • Transverse plane (also called the horizontal plane): divides the body into upper and lower parts. Rotation happens here, around the longitudinal axis (also called the vertical axis).

Many sporting movements combine planes. A golf swing is mainly rotation in the transverse plane, with flexion and extension in the sagittal plane, and a tennis serve uses all three.

Anthropometry HL

Anthropometry is the measurement of the size, shape and proportions of the human body. Common measurements include height (stature), body mass, limb and segment lengths, arm span, circumferences, skinfold thickness and body composition, and indices such as body mass index (BMI) and waist-to-hip ratio.

ApplicationHow it is used
Talent identification and selectionSome sports favour certain body dimensions, such as height and arm span in basketball and swimming, which helps coaches spot potential. It should be used with skill and fitness tests, because young athletes grow at different rates.
Equipment and workspace design (ergonomics)Bike frames, saddle height, bat and club length, and racing boat seats are fitted to body dimensions, improving efficiency and reducing injury risk.
Biomechanical analysisLimb lengths and segment masses affect leverage, torque and movement efficiency, and are used in movement analysis.
Health and growth monitoringBMI, waist circumference and body composition screen for health risks, and growth measurements track young athletes' development.
Fair competitionBody mass is used for weight categories in sports such as boxing and judo.

Anthropometric measures have limits. For example, BMI cannot tell muscle from fat, so a muscular athlete can be labelled "overweight". Results should be combined with other information.

Exam Tips

🎯
Name the joint action firstIdentify the joint and its action (for example "knee extension"), then say the plane. Don't just say "the leg moves".
📐
Axis is perpendicular to the planeSay "sagittal plane, around the frontal axis". A quick check is that the axis always points in the one direction that is not in the plane.
🏃
Describe movements from anatomical positionSay which way the body part moves relative to the anatomical position, even if the athlete is upside down mid-somersault. Check the IB command terms.
🔢
Use precise terms in a sequenceFor a skill such as a jump, list the joints in order (hip, knee, ankle) and name the action at each, such as extension, extension and plantarflexion.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't confuse planes with axes. The movement in the sagittal plane (flexion and extension) happens around the frontal axis, not the sagittal axis. The sagittal axis is for abduction and adduction.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1Define anatomical position.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • The standard reference position for describing the body: standing upright, facing forward, with arms by the sides, palms facing forward and feet forward.
2Identify the plane of movement and the axis of rotation for a biceps curl.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Sagittal plane.
  • Frontal (mediolateral) axis.
3Distinguish between the axial and the appendicular skeleton.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The axial skeleton is the skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum, and mainly supports the trunk and protects organs such as the brain, spinal cord, heart and lungs.
  • The appendicular skeleton is the limbs and the girdles that attach them (shoulder and pelvic girdles), and mainly allows movement.
4Describe the joint actions at the hip, knee and ankle during the take-off phase of a vertical jump.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Hip extension.
  • Knee extension.
  • Ankle plantarflexion. (All of these movements take place in the sagittal plane.)
5(HL) Explain two ways anthropometric measurements can be used in sport.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Talent identification: measurements such as height or arm span can show whether a young athlete has dimensions that suit a sport (for example basketball or swimming), helping to select or guide them (1 mark for use, 1 for explanation).
  • Equipment fitting and ergonomics: measurements of limb lengths are used to size equipment such as bike frames and saddle height or club length, which improves efficiency and reduces injury risk (1 mark for use, 1 for explanation).
  • Other accepted uses: weight categories, monitoring growth in young athletes, or analysing leverage in technique.
