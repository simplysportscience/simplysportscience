A.2.1 Theme A · Hydration & Nutrition SL & HL

Water & Electrolyte Balance

How the body keeps its water and electrolytes in balance, what happens when exercise, heat or drinking habits upset that balance, and how it affects performance.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

About 60% of your body mass is water, and that water carries dissolved electrolytes: charged particles such as sodium, potassium and chloride. Together they keep blood volume up, allow nerves and muscles to work, and let you cool down by sweating. Water and electrolyte balance means that what enters the body closely matches what leaves it. Exercise, heat, humidity and altitude all push the body off balance, and drinking too little or too much can harm both health and performance.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Outline how water and electrolytes enter and leave the body.
  • Describe the roles of sodium, potassium and chloride in exercise.
  • Explain how the hypothalamus, pituitary gland and kidneys regulate water balance.
  • Explain how the environment affects water and electrolyte balance in an athlete.
  • Distinguish between dehydration, hyponatraemia and hypernatraemia, and explain their effects on performance and health.
  • Outline how athletes can monitor hydration and rehydrate sensibly.

Key Terms

ElectrolyteA charged particle (ion) dissolved in body fluids, such as Na⁺, K⁺ and Cl⁻.
Water balanceThe state in which water intake matches water loss.
DehydrationA fall in total body water, caused by fluid loss exceeding fluid intake.
HyponatraemiaAbnormally low blood sodium, usually from too much water relative to sodium. (US spelling: hyponatremia.)
HypernatraemiaAbnormally high blood sodium, usually from too little water relative to sodium.
ADHAntidiuretic hormone, which makes the kidneys reabsorb more water.

Deep Dive

Why water and electrolytes matter

Water is the medium for almost everything the body does. It makes up most of blood plasma, so it carries oxygen, nutrients and hormones. It is the raw material for sweat, so it allows cooling. It is also where chemical reactions happen inside cells. Electrolytes control how water is shared between the blood, the spaces around cells and the inside of cells, because water follows sodium by osmosis. They also carry the electrical signals that nerves and muscles rely on.

ElectrolyteWhere it is mainly foundKey roles in exercise
Sodium (Na⁺)Blood plasma and the fluid around cellsSets blood volume and water distribution; needed for nerve impulses; lost in sweat
Potassium (K⁺)Inside cellsNerve impulses, muscle contraction and normal heart rhythm
Chloride (Cl⁻)Blood plasma and the fluid around cellsWorks with sodium to keep fluid balance; lost with sodium in sweat

Water and electrolyte intake and loss

Intake comes from drinks, from the water in food (fruit, vegetables, soup) and from metabolic water, a small amount produced when the body breaks down fuel. Electrolytes come from food, and from sports drinks and oral rehydration products.

Loss happens through urine, sweat, breathing (water vapour in exhaled air) and faeces. At rest, urine is the biggest loss. In exercise, sweat becomes by far the largest, and sweat carries sodium and chloride with the water (and a little potassium). Sweat rates range from about 0.5 L per hour up to 2 L per hour or more in hard exercise in the heat, and sweat sodium varies hugely between individuals, roughly 0.5 to 2 g per litre. Breathing loss also rises because ventilation increases.

Typical daily water loss for an adult at rest compared with a day including two hours of hard exercise in the heat. Values are approximate and vary between people. Notice that the body cuts urine output to conserve water.

How the environment changes the balance

  • Heat: the body sweats more to lose heat, so water and electrolyte losses rise.
  • Humidity: sweat evaporates poorly in humid air, so it drips off the skin. The athlete loses fluid and electrolytes without much cooling, and core temperature rises faster.
  • Altitude: the air is drier and breathing is faster, so more water is lost through breathing, and urine output often increases.
  • Cold: thirst is often reduced and urine output can rise, so athletes can become dehydrated without noticing.
  • Clothing and equipment: heavy kit (for example American football pads) traps heat and increases sweating.

How the body regulates water balance

Water balance is controlled by negative feedback (see A.1.2). When body water falls, the blood becomes more concentrated. Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect this and do two things. They trigger thirst, and they signal the posterior pituitary gland to release antidiuretic hormone (ADH). ADH travels in the blood to the kidneys, where it makes the collecting ducts more permeable to water. More water is reabsorbed into the blood, so less urine is produced and it is darker and more concentrated.

Sodium is controlled partly by aldosterone, released by the adrenal cortex when blood volume or sodium falls. It makes the kidneys reabsorb more sodium, and water follows by osmosis, which helps maintain blood volume.

Negative feedback control of water balance after sweat loss. ADH reduces further water loss through the kidneys, but only drinking can replace the water already lost.

When the balance is lost

Blood sodium concentration normally stays between about 135 and 145 mmol/L. Three problems matter in sport:

  • Dehydration is a fall in total body water because fluid loss exceeds intake.
  • Hypernatraemia is blood sodium above the normal range. It usually means water has been lost faster than sodium, as in dehydration, because sweat contains proportionally more water than sodium.
  • Hyponatraemia is blood sodium below the normal range. In sport it usually happens when an athlete drinks more fluid than they lose, so the sodium in the blood is diluted.
Blood sodium concentration and what happens at each end of the scale. In hyponatraemia, water moves into cells by osmosis, which is why the brain is at particular risk.

How dehydration affects performance

Performance often starts to fall once fluid loss reaches about 2% of body mass. A 70 kg athlete who finishes a hot training session weighing 68.6 kg has lost 1.4 kg, which is 2%. The main effects are:

  • Lower blood volume: plasma volume falls, so less blood returns to the heart and stroke volume drops. Heart rate rises to compensate for the same effort (cardiovascular drift), and cardiac output is harder to maintain.
  • Poorer temperature control: less blood is available to carry heat to the skin and less water is available to sweat, so core temperature rises faster.
  • Higher perceived effort: the same pace feels harder, and concentration, decision-making and skill (for example a footballer's passing) can decline.
  • Muscle problems: heavy sweating with loss of sodium and other electrolytes is linked to fatigue and cramps.

Severe dehydration with high body temperature can lead to heat illness, which is a medical emergency.

Exercise-associated hyponatraemia

Hyponatraemia is most common in long events such as marathons, ultramarathons and Ironman triathlons, particularly in slower athletes who drink at every aid station. If they drink more than they sweat, and mainly plain water, the blood sodium is diluted. Water then moves into cells by osmosis and brain cells swell, causing headache, nausea, confusion, seizures and, in severe cases, collapse. Sports drinks contain some sodium but do not fully prevent hyponatraemia if far too much is drunk. The risk is reduced by drinking according to thirst or an individual plan that matches sweat loss, rather than drinking as much as possible.

Monitoring hydration and rehydrating

MethodWhat it shows
Urine colourPale straw suggests good hydration; dark yellow suggests dehydration. Quick and cheap, but some foods and supplements change the colour.
Body mass changeWeigh before and after exercise. Each 1 kg lost is about 1 L of fluid lost. Best used to learn your own sweat rate.
ThirstA simple guide, but it lags behind actual fluid loss in hot conditions, and can be switched off by excitement or cold.
Urine specific gravityMeasures how concentrated the urine is. More accurate, but needs equipment.
  • Before: start well hydrated, drinking steadily in the hours before rather than gulping just before the start.
  • During: replace some of the sweat loss, matched to the individual, aiming to limit body mass loss to under about 2% without overdrinking. Add sodium in long or very hot events.
  • After: replace more than was lost (about 125–150% of the body mass lost) over the following hours, together with sodium from food or drinks, because urine continues to be produced.

Exam Tips

🎯
Name the ion and the direction of change"Plasma sodium falls below 135 mmol/L" scores. "Electrolytes are lost" doesn't. Always say which electrolyte, and whether it rises or falls.
💧
Link the cause to the effect through osmosisFor hyponatraemia, write that diluted blood makes water move into cells by osmosis, so cells (including brain cells) swell. That is the mechanism the mark scheme wants.
🌡️
Give a mechanism for environment questions"It's hot" isn't enough. Say why it matters: more sweating, poorer evaporation in humid air, or more breathing loss at altitude. Check the IB command terms.
🏃
Use the sport and the numbersA marathon runner in heat, or a 2% body mass loss (1.4 kg for a 70 kg athlete), shows you can apply the idea.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't say hyponatraemia is caused by "losing too much salt in sweat". The main cause is drinking too much fluid relative to sodium, which dilutes the blood. Sweat sodium loss can contribute, but dilution is the key point.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1Define dehydration.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • A reduction in total body water, which occurs when fluid loss is greater than fluid intake.
2Outline two ways in which an athlete loses water during exercise.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Sweat: water is lost as sweat evaporates to cool the body (along with sodium and chloride).
  • Breathing: water vapour is lost in exhaled air, and this increases as ventilation rises.
  • Urine (reduced during exercise) is also accepted. Any two.
3Outline the role of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) in maintaining water balance.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The hypothalamus detects that the blood has become more concentrated (body water has fallen).
  • The posterior pituitary gland releases ADH into the blood.
  • ADH makes the kidneys reabsorb more water, so less, more concentrated urine is produced.
4Explain how exercising in hot, humid conditions affects an athlete's water and electrolyte balance.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Heat increases the sweat rate, so more water, sodium and chloride are lost.
  • High humidity reduces evaporation, so sweat drips off without cooling, and the athlete sweats even more to try to lose heat.
  • If fluid intake does not match these losses, the athlete becomes dehydrated, and blood volume and plasma sodium balance are disrupted.
5Explain how hyponatraemia can develop in a marathon runner and how it affects health and performance.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The runner drinks more fluid than they lose in sweat, especially plain water with little sodium.
  • This dilutes the blood, so plasma sodium falls below the normal range (about 135 mmol/L).
  • Water moves into cells by osmosis, so cells, including brain cells, swell.
  • This causes headache, nausea, confusion and, in severe cases, seizures and collapse, so performance falls and health is at risk.
