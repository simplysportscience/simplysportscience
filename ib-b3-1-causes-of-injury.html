B.3.1 Theme B · Injury SL & HL

Causes of Injury

Why injuries happen: the risk factors that predispose an athlete, the mechanisms that cause acute and cumulative injuries, and how injury risk is assessed.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Injuries are rarely caused by one thing. They usually happen when several risk factors build up in one athlete, and then an event, the mechanism of injury, applies a load the tissue cannot tolerate. Risk factors are split into internal (within the athlete, such as previous injury, fitness or technique) and external (from the environment, such as training errors, equipment or the playing surface). Injuries are either acute, from one sudden event, or cumulative (overuse), from repeated stress over time. At higher level, you link overuse injury to technique and biomechanical maladaptation.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Distinguish between internal and external risk factors, with examples from sport.
  • Explain how a risk factor predisposes an athlete to injury before the injuring event.
  • Describe the mechanisms of acute and cumulative (overuse) injuries and give examples of each.
  • Explain how risk can be assessed using screening and a risk matrix.
  • HL: Explain how poor technique and biomechanical maladaptations can lead to chronic or overuse injuries.

Key Terms

Risk factorA variable that makes an individual more likely to be injured.
Internal risk factorA factor within the individual, such as age, sex, fitness, previous injury or technique.
External risk factorA factor outside the individual, such as training errors, equipment, surfaces or weather.
Acute injuryAn injury caused by one sudden event, such as a sprain, strain, fracture or dislocation.
Cumulative (overuse) injuryAn injury that develops gradually from repeated stress, such as tendinopathy or a stress fracture.
Biomechanical maladaptation (HL)A habitual movement or technique fault that places repeated, excessive stress on tissues.

Deep Dive

Risk factors: a multifactorial picture

When analysing an injury, avoid blaming one cause. Instead, show how risk factors combine. Internal risk factors make a person more susceptible to injury, and external risk factors are variables in the environment. The model below shows how they lead to injury.

A simple model of injury. Internal and external risk factors together predispose an athlete to injury. A mechanism of injury (the inciting event) then produces either an acute or a cumulative injury.

To identify risk factors from a scenario, separate the predisposing factors (those already present before the event) from the trauma that actually causes the injury. For example, a footballer with a previous ankle sprain who lands on another player's foot is predisposed by the old injury, and the landing on another player's foot is the mechanism.

Mechanisms of injury: acute and cumulative

An acute injury comes from one identifiable event, usually with a clear onset. Typical acute injuries are sprains (ligament damage), strains (muscle or tendon damage), fractures, dislocations, contusions (bruises), lacerations (cuts) and concussion. A cumulative (overuse) injury develops slowly as a tissue is repeatedly loaded faster than it can repair. Typical cumulative injuries are tendinopathy, stress fractures and some shin pain.

Tissue load against time for the two types of injury (schematic). Acute injury happens when one load exceeds tolerance. Cumulative injury happens when repeated loads wear tolerance down until a load that was previously tolerated causes damage.

The distinction matters for prevention. Acute injuries are reduced through safer techniques, equipment, rules and awareness of risk. Cumulative injuries are reduced by managing load: building training gradually, including recovery, and changing the stresses placed on the body. Many coaches avoid sudden large increases in training load, although the evidence for an exact weekly percentage is limited, so the rule should be applied to the individual.

Assessing injury risk

Coaches and clinicians assess risk in two ways. Screening and fitness testing identify athletes with weaknesses, imbalances or poor movement patterns before injury occurs, for example through a range-of-motion test, a strength test or a landing assessment. A risk matrix ranks hazards by how likely they are and how severe the injury would be, so that the most important risks are dealt with first.

A risk matrix. Risk is the product of likelihood and severity (each scored 1 to 3). Hazards in the high-risk cells are controlled first. Examples are illustrative.

Technique and overuse injury HL

Many chronic injuries are linked to technique. A movement fault that is repeated thousands of times, such as an overstriding runner or a bowler with a poor front-foot landing, places large and repeated forces on the same tissues. Over time these tissues become overloaded and injured. This is a biomechanical maladaptation: the body adapts to the fault in a way that increases stress on a tissue.

Example chain: knee valgus (the knee collapsing inwards) on landing increases strain on the anterior cruciate ligament, so repeated landings with valgus can lead to ligament damage and, over time, knee pain. The predisposing factors might be weak hip muscles and fatigue, and the technique fault is the mechanism.

For HL answers, explain how correcting biomechanical maladaptations reduces risk. Video analysis, force plates or coach observation can identify the fault, and drills, feedback and strength work can change the movement pattern. Changing technique reduces the repeated stress on the tissue, which allows it to recover.

Psychological influences on injury risk

Stress and anxiety can increase muscle tension and reduce control, while high arousal can narrow attention and lead to mistakes. Athletes who have experienced injury may return to play with fear, which can change their movement pattern and increase the risk of re-injury. Coaches can reduce these risks through good communication, managed training loads and psychological support.

Exam Tips

🔗
Use the chain: risk factor → mechanism → tissue → outcomeFor example: previous ankle sprain → reduced proprioception → unstable landing → ligament sprain. This structure scores well on causation questions.
⚖️
Separate predisposing factors from the traumaSay what made the athlete more likely to be injured, then say what event caused the injury. Check the IB command terms.
🏃
Classify the factor and name the sport"Internal: muscle imbalance between the quadriceps and hamstrings, which increases strain on the knee in sprinting." Name the type and the example.
🧩
For HL, tie technique to tissueDescribe the fault, the repeated stress it causes, the tissue affected, and how correcting the technique reduces the risk.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't blame injury on one factor, such as "too much training". Injuries are multifactorial. Also don't confuse the risk factor with the mechanism: fatigue is a risk factor; a collision is a mechanism.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1State one example of an internal risk factor for injury.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • Any one of: previous injury, low strength or flexibility, muscle imbalance, age, sex, fatigue or poor technique.
2Distinguish between an acute injury and a cumulative (overuse) injury.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • An acute injury results from a single, sudden event (for example a sprain from a twisting landing).
  • A cumulative injury develops gradually from repeated stress that exceeds the tissue's ability to recover (for example tendinopathy).
3Identify one external risk factor and explain how it could predispose an athlete to injury.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • For example, a wet or uneven playing surface, poor footwear or a sudden increase in training load.
  • It reduces grip and stability, or overloads tissues before they can adapt, making injury more likely.
4Explain how a previous ankle sprain can predispose a footballer to a further sprain.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The previous injury can reduce proprioception (joint position sense) and control around the ankle.
  • This makes landing or changing direction less stable, so the ankle is more likely to roll.
  • The sudden, uncontrolled landing (the mechanism) then causes a further ligament sprain.
5(HL) Explain how poor technique can lead to a chronic (overuse) injury, and how a coach could reduce this risk.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • A repeated technique fault, such as knee valgus on landing, places repeated excessive stress on a tissue (for example the anterior cruciate ligament).
  • Over time the tissue is overloaded faster than it can repair, causing damage and chronic injury.
  • The coach can identify the fault (for example through video analysis) and use drills, feedback and strength work to correct the movement pattern.
  • Correcting the biomechanical maladaptation reduces repeated stress, so the tissue has time to recover.
