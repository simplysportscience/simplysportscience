A.2.2 Theme A · Hydration & Nutrition SL & HL

Fuelling for Health & Performance

How carbohydrate, fat and protein fuel the body at rest and in exercise, how to time and plan fuelling, and what happens when energy intake is too low.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Food is the fuel and the building material for the body. The three macronutrients, carbohydrate, fat and protein, supply energy. Which one the body uses most depends on how hard and how long you exercise, and on how well fuelled you are. Athletes can improve performance by timing what they eat around training and competition, but eating too little for the energy they use causes low energy availability, which harms health and performance. At higher level you also study micronutrients and the gut microbiome.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Outline the roles, energy values and food sources of carbohydrate, fat and protein.
  • Explain how exercise intensity, duration and other factors change which fuel is used.
  • Describe fuelling strategies before, during and after exercise, including carbohydrate loading.
  • Explain what low energy availability is, and how it affects health and performance.
  • HL: Explain the roles of micronutrients in energy transfer and tissue synthesis.
  • HL: Outline how the gut microbiome influences health and performance.

Key Terms

MacronutrientA nutrient needed in large amounts that provides energy: carbohydrate, fat or protein.
GlycogenThe stored form of carbohydrate, held in muscle and the liver.
Carbohydrate loadingRaising muscle and liver glycogen above normal before a long event.
Low energy availability (LEA)Too little energy left for normal body functions after the cost of exercise.
Micronutrient (HL)A vitamin or mineral needed in small amounts; it does not provide energy itself.
Gut microbiome (HL)The community of microbes living in the digestive tract.

Deep Dive

The three macronutrients

MacronutrientEnergy per gramMain rolesTypical sources
Carbohydrateabout 4 kcal (17 kJ)Main fuel for moderate to high intensity exercise; stored as glycogen in muscle and liver; the brain's preferred fuelRice, pasta, bread, oats, fruit, potatoes
Fat (lipid)about 9 kcal (37 kJ)Main fuel at rest and low intensity; very large stores; absorbs vitamins A, D, E and K; protects organsOils, nuts, oily fish, avocado, dairy
Proteinabout 4 kcal (17 kJ)Growth and repair of tissue, enzymes and hormones; a small energy source, larger in long exercise or when carbohydrate is lowMeat, fish, eggs, dairy, beans, tofu

Daily needs depend on training load. Endurance athletes may need roughly 3 to 12 g of carbohydrate per kg of body mass per day, from light to very heavy training, and about 1.2 to 2.0 g of protein per kg per day, with fat making up roughly 20 to 35% of energy intake.

Which fuel is used? Intensity and duration

The body always uses a mixture of fuels. At rest and at low intensity, fat supplies the biggest share, because the demand for ATP is low and fat can be broken down slowly using oxygen. As intensity rises, ATP is needed faster. Carbohydrate can be broken down much more quickly, and it can supply energy both with and without oxygen, so its share rises. At high intensity it provides most of the energy. Protein supplies only a small share (about 5%) throughout.

Approximate share of energy from each macronutrient. The exact mix varies with diet, fitness and the individual, but carbohydrate use always rises with intensity.

Body fat stores are huge, but glycogen stores are small: roughly 2000 kcal in muscle and liver combined, enough for about 90 to 120 minutes of hard endurance exercise. When glycogen runs low, power output, speed and concentration fall, which marathon runners call "hitting the wall". This is why carbohydrate availability matters so much for performance.

Other factors that influence fuel use include:

  • Duration: in long exercise, glycogen is gradually used up and the body relies more on fat (and a little more protein).
  • Training status: trained endurance athletes use more fat at a given intensity, which spares glycogen.
  • Diet: a high-carbohydrate diet raises glycogen stores and carbohydrate use; low carbohydrate availability shifts the body towards fat and protein.
  • Sex: on average, women use slightly more fat and less carbohydrate than men at the same relative intensity, but the differences between individuals are larger than the difference between sexes. Sex-related factors also affect energy availability and how the gut responds to eating during exercise.
  • Environment: exercise in the heat tends to increase carbohydrate use.

Fuelling strategies

The aim is to start exercise with enough carbohydrate available, to keep it topped up if the event is long, and to refuel afterwards, while avoiding stomach problems. Athletes should practise their strategy in training, never try new foods on competition day.

Typical carbohydrate guidelines for an endurance athlete. Amounts are approximate and are individualised using body mass, event length and gut tolerance.
  • Carbohydrate loading only helps events lasting longer than about 90 minutes, such as a marathon or a long cycling race. It raises glycogen stores so they last longer. It gives no benefit in a 100 m sprint.
  • Glycaemic index (GI): high GI foods raise blood glucose quickly and are useful during and straight after exercise. Low GI foods release glucose more slowly and are often chosen in the pre-exercise meal. Total intake, timing and tolerance matter as much as GI.
  • After exercise: carbohydrate restores glycogen, and protein supplies amino acids for muscle repair and adaptation.

Low energy availability and RED-S

Energy availability is the energy left for the body's normal functions after the energy cost of exercise has been subtracted from energy intake, expressed per kg of fat-free mass. Low energy availability (LEA) occurs when this is too low to support those functions. It can be unintentional (not realising how much energy training uses) or deliberate (dieting, or pressure to be lean in sports such as gymnastics, distance running or weight-class sports). It affects both female and male athletes. Prolonged LEA can lead to Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S).

Worked example: an athlete with 50 kg of fat-free mass eats 2300 kcal per day and uses 800 kcal in training. Energy availability = (2300 − 800) ÷ 50 = 30 kcal per kg of fat-free mass per day, right at the threshold below which problems become likely.

How energy availability is calculated, commonly used thresholds (these vary slightly between sources), and the body systems affected by prolonged low energy availability.

Micronutrients HL

Vitamins and minerals are needed in small amounts. They do not provide energy themselves. Instead, they have specific jobs in energy transfer (releasing and using the energy in food) and tissue synthesis (building and repairing tissue).

MicronutrientRoleIf intake is too low
IronPart of haemoglobin and myoglobin (oxygen transport) and of enzymes in aerobic energy productionAnaemia, fatigue, lower aerobic capacity
B vitaminsCoenzymes in the release of energy from carbohydrate, fat and protein; B12 and folate help make red blood cellsFatigue, poor recovery, anaemia
Vitamin CNeeded to make collagen for tendons, ligaments and skin; antioxidantSlower tissue repair
Calcium and vitamin DBone formation; calcium is also needed for muscle contraction, and vitamin D helps absorb calciumWeaker bones, stress fractures
MagnesiumHelps enzymes that use ATP; muscle and nerve functionCramps, fatigue
ZincProtein synthesis, tissue repair and immune functionSlower recovery, more infections

A varied diet usually provides enough. Supplements are most useful where a deficiency has been confirmed (for example iron or vitamin D) or diet is restricted (for example vitamin B12 for vegans). Taking more than needed gives no extra benefit, can be harmful (for example iron overload), and supplements can be contaminated with banned substances.

The gut microbiome HL

Trillions of microbes live in the digestive tract, mostly in the large intestine. They ferment fibre to produce short-chain fatty acids, which feed the cells lining the gut and have anti-inflammatory effects. They also influence how nutrients are absorbed, immune function, inflammation and, through the gut–brain connection, mood. A healthy, diverse microbiome can therefore support fuel availability, recovery, immune health and general health. Very hard, prolonged exercise can cause stomach and bowel problems in some athletes, and a diet rich in varied plant foods and fibre supports a diverse microbiome. Research is still developing, and evidence that probiotic supplements improve performance is mixed.

Exam Tips

🎯
Say how the fuel mix changes, not "which fuel"Fat is the main fuel at rest and low intensity; carbohydrate takes over as intensity rises. Never write that fat is used only at low intensity, because both are used all the time.
🔬
Use the exact termWrite "glycogen" for stored carbohydrate and "blood glucose" for carbohydrate in the blood. Say "energy availability", not just "not eating enough".
⏱️
For fuelling strategies, give timing, amount and reason"Eat about 1–4 g/kg of carbohydrate 1–4 hours before, to top up glycogen" scores. "Eat carbs before" doesn't. Check the IB command terms.
🏃
Match the strategy to the sportCarbohydrate loading suits a marathon or long road race, not a 100 m sprint or a 60-minute training run. Showing this is how you earn application marks.
⚠️
Common mistake (HL)Don't write that vitamins and minerals "give you energy". Micronutrients contain no usable energy. They help release energy from macronutrients and help build and repair tissue.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1Define macronutrient.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • A nutrient needed in large amounts that provides energy, i.e. carbohydrate, fat (lipid) or protein.
2Outline two reasons why carbohydrate is the main fuel during high-intensity exercise.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Carbohydrate can be broken down quickly, so ATP is resynthesised fast enough for high-intensity work.
  • It can supply energy both with and without oxygen (anaerobically and aerobically).
  • It is stored as glycogen in the muscles themselves, so it is immediately available.
  • Any two.
3Describe how the contributions of carbohydrate and fat to energy supply change as exercise intensity increases.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • At rest and at low intensity, fat provides the largest share of energy.
  • As intensity increases, the contribution of carbohydrate increases and the contribution of fat decreases.
  • At high intensity, carbohydrate is the dominant fuel, while protein remains a small contributor throughout.
4Explain the purpose of carbohydrate loading and identify one type of event for which it is suitable.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Carbohydrate loading increases glycogen stores in the muscles and liver above normal levels.
  • This delays glycogen depletion and fatigue, so the athlete can maintain intensity for longer.
  • It is suitable for events lasting longer than about 90 minutes, such as a marathon.
5Explain how low energy availability can affect the health and performance of an athlete.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Low energy availability means energy intake is too low to cover the cost of exercise and still support normal body functions.
  • It can reduce bone density and increase the risk of stress fractures.
  • It can disrupt hormones (for example menstrual cycles in females or testosterone in males) and weaken immune function, leading to more illness.
  • Performance falls because of reduced endurance and strength, and recovery is slower.
