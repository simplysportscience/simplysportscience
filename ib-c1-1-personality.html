C.1.1 Theme C · Individual Differences SL & HL

Personality

What personality is, how it is described and measured, how it interacts with the situation, and why no personality profile predicts sport performance on its own.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Personality is the set of characteristic patterns of thinking, feeling and behaviour that make one person different from another. Psychologists explain behaviour in three main ways. The trait approach sees personality as stable characteristics that drive behaviour across situations. The situational approach sees behaviour as shaped mainly by the environment, including what others do. The interactionist approach sees behaviour as the result of both. In sport, the most widely used trait framework is the Big Five. Athletes also learn behaviours by observing role models, and personality can change over time through experience and reflection.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Explain the trait-based approach and why traits are considered relatively stable.
  • Identify and apply all five Big Five traits to sport and exercise examples.
  • Explain social learning theory and apply it to a sporting example.
  • Explain how personality can change through experience and reflection over time.
  • Outline how personality is measured in sport, and the limitations of measurement.
  • HL: Evaluate why no single personality profile predicts sport performance.

Key Terms

PersonalityCharacteristic patterns of thinking, feeling and behaviour that differ between individuals.
TraitA relatively stable characteristic that shows up in behaviour across many situations.
Big FiveA trait model of five dimensions: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism.
Interactionist approachThe view that behaviour results from the interaction of personality and the situation.
Social learning theoryThe view that behaviour is learned by observing and imitating models.
ModelA person whose behaviour is observed and copied, such as a coach, senior player or role model.

Deep Dive

Three approaches to behaviour

The trait approach describes personality as a set of relatively enduring characteristics. If an athlete is consistently competitive across training, matches and social situations, the trait view explains this as a stable tendency. Trait approaches describe broad, stable patterns of behaviour; they do not predict behaviour perfectly in every case.

The situational approach argues that the situation is the main influence on behaviour. A player who is aggressive on the pitch but calm in class is responding to the context, not showing a fixed trait. The interactionist approach combines the two: the same person may respond very differently depending on the situation, and different people respond differently to the same situation.

Three explanations of sporting behaviour. The interactionist approach, shown at the bottom, is often considered the most balanced view.

The Big Five in sport

The Big Five is the most widely used trait model. Each dimension is a continuum, so people sit at different points on each one. The table shows how each trait may be relevant to sport and exercise.

The Big Five traits, with a description and illustrative sporting examples at high and low levels. These are tendencies, not guarantees of behaviour.
TraitPossible effect on sporting behaviour
OpennessWillingness to learn new tactics or training methods; may be more adaptable to changing coaching
ConscientiousnessConsistency in training, preparation and following instructions; discipline in recovery
ExtraversionComfort in team settings, communication and leadership; may find long solitary training harder
AgreeablenessCooperation, supporting teammates and resolving conflict within a team
NeuroticismTendency to experience anxiety and worry, which can affect performance under pressure (see C.4.1)

Social learning theory

According to social learning theory (Bandura), people learn many behaviours by watching others and copying them, not only through their own experience. Learning from a model depends on four processes: attention (noticing the behaviour), retention (remembering it), reproduction (physically being able to copy it) and motivation (wanting to copy it, often because it is rewarded or admired).

Social learning theory applied to sport. A model's behaviour is copied only if the learner attends to it, remembers it, can reproduce it and is motivated to do so.

In sport this explains how young athletes learn effort, discipline and sporting behaviour from coaches, senior players and professional role models. It also means that coaches and older teammates influence the behaviour of the whole group, so it matters what they model.

Long-term changes in personality

Personality is relatively stable, but it is not fixed. Long-term sport participation can shape behaviours such as discipline, confidence, emotional control and cooperation. This happens through experience (repeatedly facing challenges and setbacks) and reflection (thinking about performance and deliberately changing how one responds). An athlete who learns to manage nerves before competition, for example, may become more emotionally controlled over several seasons.

This change challenges the idea that traits are completely fixed. A balanced view is that core traits are relatively stable across time, while behaviour and how a person handles situations can develop.

Measuring personality in sport

Personality is usually measured with self-report questionnaires, in which athletes rate statements about themselves. These are quick and easy to use, and they can be given to many people. However, they have limitations:

  • Social desirability bias: athletes may answer in the way they think is expected or admirable.
  • Situational specificity: a trait measured in general may not predict behaviour in a specific competitive situation.
  • Response consistency: answers can change with mood, recent results or the setting in which the test is given, so reliability matters.
  • Ethics: personality data is personal. It should be collected with informed consent, used only for stated purposes, kept confidential and not used to exclude athletes from selection.

Personality and performance HL

A common idea is that elite athletes share a particular personality profile. Research has not supported this: no single personality profile predicts sport performance. Athletes in the same sport can have very different personalities, and the same personality can lead to different results in different situations. Performance depends on many factors, including skill, fitness, coaching, tactics, opportunity, health and the situation, and personality interacts with these.

Personality is still useful. It can help coaches understand how an athlete is likely to respond to feedback, pressure or a team role, and help them tailor support. The key evaluation is that personality should inform individual support, not be used as a fixed predictor of success or as the reason for choosing or rejecting an athlete.

Exam Tips

🎯
Name the approach firstFor "explain" questions, say whether you are using the trait, situational or interactionist approach, then apply it to the sport in the question.
🔢
Apply, don't listName the Big Five trait and give a sporting effect, for example "high conscientiousness helps an athlete stick to a demanding training plan". Check the IB command terms.
🧠
Use Bandura's four processesFor social learning questions, name attention, retention, reproduction and motivation, and say which one is relevant to the example.
⚖️
Evaluate with limitationsFor measurement or performance questions, give social desirability, situational specificity or the lack of a predictive profile as a limitation.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't say a particular personality type guarantees success, or that elite athletes all share one profile. Also, traits are tendencies, not fixed behaviour.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1Define the term trait.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • A relatively stable characteristic of a person that influences their behaviour across many different situations.
2Identify two of the Big Five personality dimensions and give a sporting example of each.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Conscientiousness: a disciplined athlete who follows a training plan consistently.
  • Extraversion: a sociable athlete who thrives in a team role and communicates well with teammates. (Any two dimensions with a valid example.)
3Outline the interactionist approach to personality, using a sporting example.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Behaviour results from the interaction of personality (individual characteristics) and the situation (environment and demands).
  • The same athlete may behave differently in different situations, and different athletes may respond differently to the same situation.
  • Example: a nervous athlete may perform more calmly when a supportive coach is present than in a highly critical environment.
4Explain how social learning theory can explain a young athlete adopting the behaviour of a respected senior player.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Attention: the young athlete notices the senior player's behaviour, which is admired and similar to their own situation.
  • Retention: the athlete remembers the behaviour, for example through observation and mental rehearsal.
  • Reproduction: the athlete has the physical ability to copy the behaviour through practice.
  • Motivation: the athlete wants to copy it, because it is rewarded or valued by the team and coaches.
5(HL) Evaluate the view that personality can be used to predict sporting success.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • No single personality profile has been shown to predict sport performance, and athletes in the same sport often have very different personalities.
  • Performance depends on many factors, including skill, fitness, coaching, tactics and the situation, and personality interacts with these.
  • Self-report measures have limitations, such as social desirability bias and situational specificity, which make prediction less reliable.
  • Personality is still useful for understanding how an athlete responds to pressure or feedback, but it should not be used as a fixed predictor or to exclude athletes.
