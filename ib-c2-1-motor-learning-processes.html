C.2.1 Theme C · Motor Learning SL & HL

Motor Learning Processes

How people acquire skills through practice and feedback: the information processing model, the three stages of learning, and how practice design supports transfer.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Motor learning is a relatively permanent change in the ability to perform a skill, brought about by practice and experience. It is different from performance, which can change temporarily because of fatigue, mood or conditions. The information processing model describes how a performer takes in information, decides what to do, produces a movement and uses feedback to improve the next attempt. Fitts and Posner describe three stages through which learners typically progress: cognitive, associative and autonomous. Coaches use these ideas to choose feedback, practice and cues that suit the learner's stage.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Distinguish between motor learning and performance.
  • Describe the information processing model and apply it to a sporting skill.
  • Describe the cognitive, associative and autonomous stages of learning, and their characteristics.
  • Explain intrinsic and extrinsic (augmented) feedback, including knowledge of results and knowledge of performance.
  • Explain how practice design (massed or distributed, constant or variable) affects learning and transfer.
  • Apply these ideas to plan a coaching session for a learner at a given stage.

Key Terms

Motor learningA relatively permanent change in the ability to perform a skill, brought about by practice.
Information processingTaking in information, deciding what to do, producing movement and using feedback.
Intrinsic feedbackInformation the performer gets from their own senses during or after a movement.
Augmented (extrinsic) feedbackInformation from outside the performer, such as a coach's comment, video or the result.
TransferThe influence of previous learning on the learning or performance of a new skill.
Variable practicePractising the same skill under changing conditions, which supports transfer to new situations.

Deep Dive

Motor learning and performance

Learning is inferred from a relatively permanent change, which is why it is measured after practice, often in a retention test or a transfer test, rather than during practice. Performance is the temporary level shown on a particular day. A player may perform well in training because the conditions are easy, without having learned the skill. Coaches therefore design practice to produce learning, not just good performance during practice.

The information processing model

The information processing model treats the performer as a system that moves through a sequence of stages. A performer takes in information from the environment, decides what to do, produces the movement, and receives feedback that updates the next attempt. Experienced performers process information more efficiently: they notice relevant cues quickly and need less decision time.

The information processing model applied to a volleyball serve. Feedback from each attempt updates the next input, decision and movement.

The three stages of learning (Fitts and Posner)

Fitts and Posner describe learning as three overlapping stages. Learners move through them at different speeds, and they may be at different stages for different skills.

  • Cognitive stage: the learner works out what the skill involves. They think about each cue and body position, make many errors and feel clumsy. Coaching should use simple, clear cues, demonstrations, basic feedback and safe, low-pressure practice.
  • Associative stage: the learner refines the skill. Errors fall, timing and consistency improve, and the learner starts to detect and correct their own mistakes. Coaching should give more specific feedback, vary the practice and encourage error detection.
  • Autonomous stage: the skill becomes largely automatic. It needs less attention, so the performer can focus on tactics, adapt to pressure and combine skills. Coaching should challenge the performer with changing and competitive conditions.
The Fitts and Posner stages, with errors falling over time (a typical pattern, illustrative). The focus of coaching changes at each stage.

Note that the autonomous stage is not always fully reached. A skill may appear automatic but break down under distraction or fatigue, so performance under pressure should be tested.

Feedback

Feedback is information about a movement that helps the learner improve. It can be intrinsic, coming from the performer's own senses (proprioception, kinaesthesis, vision), or extrinsic, coming from outside. Extrinsic feedback is often called augmented feedback, and it includes:

  • Knowledge of results (KR): information about the outcome of the movement, such as whether the shot went in or the time achieved.
  • Knowledge of performance (KP): information about how the movement was made, such as the position of the elbow or the body shape at take-off, often from video or coach observation.

The learner's stage affects what feedback is useful. Beginners often need specific, frequent feedback on the form of the movement, because they cannot yet judge it. As the learner develops, too much augmented feedback can make them dependent on the coach, so feedback should be reduced and the learner encouraged to judge their own performance. This is known as the guidance or frequency effect: frequent feedback helps in the short term, but it may hinder long-term learning if it prevents the learner building their own error detection.

Intrinsic and extrinsic feedback, and the two main types of practice design. Massed and distributed practice differ in the rest between repetitions; constant and variable practice differ in whether conditions change.

Practice and transfer

How practice is organised affects how well a skill is learned:

  • Massed practice (repetitions with short rest) can improve performance quickly in the short term, but may lead to fatigue and less retention.
  • Distributed practice (repetitions spread out with longer rest, or across several sessions) usually gives better retention and long-term learning.
  • Constant practice (the same conditions every time) helps early learning, because the performer can build a clear movement pattern.
  • Variable practice (changing conditions, such as different targets or opponents) helps learners build flexible patterns that transfer to new situations.

Transfer is the influence of previous learning on a new skill. Positive transfer occurs when the earlier skill helps the new one, for example a tennis player learning badminton. Negative transfer occurs when the earlier skill interferes, for example a badminton player's shuttle-hitting action getting in the way of learning a tennis serve. Variable practice and clear links between skills help positive transfer.

Planning a coaching session

To plan a session, identify the learner's stage first, then choose the features that suit it. A beginner in the cognitive stage may need a demonstration, one or two simple cues, short sets with rest, and plenty of positive, specific KR. An athlete in the associative stage may need more varied practice, feedback on the key details of the movement, and time to detect their own errors. An autonomous performer may practise under pressure, with tactical decisions added.

Exam Tips

🎯
Name the stage and its characteristicsFor stage questions, give the stage name and two features of it, then apply them to the sport. Check the IB command terms.
🔁
Match feedback to the stageBeginners need specific, frequent feedback on form. More advanced learners need less, so they can detect their own errors.
📚
Use the correct feedback termSay "knowledge of results" for the outcome and "knowledge of performance" for how the movement was made. Intrinsic feedback comes from the performer.
🔀
Link practice to transferVariable practice supports transfer to new situations. Distributed practice usually gives better retention than massed practice.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't confuse performance with learning. A good performance in practice does not prove that learning has occurred. Also, don't say that more feedback is always better.

2State two characteristics of the associative stage of learning.2 marksAnswer

  • Timing improves and the learner starts to detect and correct their own errors. (Any two.)
3Distinguish between knowledge of results and knowledge of performance.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Knowledge of results is information about the outcome of the movement (for example whether the shot went in).
  • Knowledge of performance is information about how the movement was produced (for example the position of the elbow).
4Explain why a coach might use distributed practice rather than massed practice when teaching a new skill.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Distributed practice spreads repetitions out with longer rest, which reduces fatigue during practice.
  • This usually leads to better retention and long-term learning than massed practice, which can improve short-term performance but may not last.
  • Distributed practice therefore supports learning rather than just good performance in the session.
5Discuss how a coach could adjust feedback and practice for a beginner in the cognitive stage compared with an athlete in the autonomous stage.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Beginner (cognitive stage): simple cues and demonstrations, frequent specific feedback on form, short sessions with safe, low-pressure practice.
  • Autonomous stage: less frequent feedback, so the athlete can detect their own errors, and practice that challenges them with pressure, tactics and variable conditions.
  • Feedback is reduced as the learner develops, to avoid dependence on the coach and to build intrinsic error detection.
  • Coaches should check that the skill holds up under pressure and distraction, not just in calm conditions.
