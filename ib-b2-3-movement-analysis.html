B.2.3 Theme B · Forces, Motion & Movement SL & HL

Movement Analysis & Its Applications

How coaches, scientists and therapists break a movement into phases, measure it and use the results to improve technique, reduce injury risk and guide rehabilitation.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Movement analysis means breaking a movement down so it can be described, measured and improved. Analysts divide a skill into phases (preparation, force production, the critical instant, and follow-through or recovery) and then judge each phase by qualitative methods (careful observation) and quantitative methods (numerical measurement with video, motion capture, force plates, wearable sensors or EMG). The results are used to improve technique, spot movement patterns that raise injury risk, and guide rehabilitation, for example through gait analysis.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Describe the phases of movement for discrete and continuous skills.
  • Distinguish between qualitative and quantitative analysis.
  • Outline the main tools used to analyse movement and what each measures.
  • Interpret simple data such as a force plate graph.
  • Explain how movement analysis is used to improve performance, reduce injury risk and guide rehabilitation.

Key Terms

Movement analysisBreaking down a movement so that it can be described, measured and improved.
Critical instantThe key moment of a skill, such as ball contact or release, that decides the outcome.

Quantitative analysisMeasuring movement with numbers, such as angles, speed or force.

Deep Dive

Phases of movement

Breaking a skill into phases makes it easier to see where a problem starts. For a discrete skill (one with a clear start and end, such as a kick, a throw or a jump) the phases are:

  • Preparation: sets up the movement, with a stable stance, good balance and a backswing or wind-up that lets the muscles stretch.
  • Force production: the main movement, where muscles create force. The timing and sequence of joint actions, range of motion and the direction of force are key.
  • Critical instant: the key moment that decides the outcome, such as ball contact in a kick or release in a javelin throw.
  • Follow-through: the limb slows down safely after the critical instant, and its path shows the quality of the technique.

For a continuous skill such as running, swimming or cycling, the movement repeats in a cycle, so the final phase is a recovery phase that returns the body to the start of the next cycle.

The phases of a football kick (a discrete skill). For each phase the analyst looks at different features, such as balance and posture in preparation and joint angles at the critical instant.

Analysis can target joint position, timing, balance, coordination, range of motion, force application or body alignment. For example, in a tennis serve the preparation is the ball toss and the trophy position, the force production is the leg drive and arm swing, the critical instant is contact at full reach, and the follow-through is the racket slowing as it crosses the body.

Qualitative and quantitative analysis

QualitativeQuantitative
What it isObservation and description of movement quality, often by a coach, teacher or therapistNumerical measurement of movement, such as angles, speed, time and force
Example"The elbow drops before release and the follow-through is short."Elbow angle 95° at release; ball speed 24 m/s; contact time 0.18 s
StrengthsQuick, cheap, needs no equipment, gives immediate feedbackObjective, accurate and repeatable, so it can track progress
LimitationsSubjective and affected by the observer's experience and biasCostly, needs expertise and equipment, and may be hard to use in competition

The two approaches work best together: observation spots what to measure, and data confirms and quantifies it.

Tools for analysing movement

Some tools measure kinematics (the motion itself: positions, angles, velocity) and some measure kinetics (the forces causing it).

Common methods of movement analysis, what each measures and a key limitation. Observation is qualitative; the others are quantitative.

Using a force plate: a vertical jump

A force plate records the ground reaction force over time. In a countermovement jump, the graph shows the athlete first standing at body weight, then unweighting (force drops below body weight as they dip), then propulsion (force rises well above body weight to accelerate upwards), then flight (no contact, so zero force) and finally landing (a high force peak). Analysts can read off peak force, impulse and, from the flight time, jump height using h = g × t² ÷ 8.

Force–time graph of a countermovement jump for a 75 kg athlete (illustrative). A flight time of about 0.38 s gives a jump height of about 0.18 m.

Applications of movement analysis

  • Improving technique and performance: comparing an athlete's movement with a model technique, or with their own previous data. For example, measuring release angle and speed in the shot put, or stroke rate in swimming.
  • Reducing injury risk: finding movement patterns linked to injury, such as poor landing mechanics, the knee collapsing inwards (valgus) on landing, limited range of motion, a large difference between the two legs, or poor postural control.
  • Rehabilitation and return to play: tracking recovery with objective measures such as jump force symmetry, range of motion and gait, and using them to decide when an athlete can safely return.
  • Monitoring load: wearable sensors track distance, speed and accelerations in training and matches, helping coaches manage fatigue and injury risk.

Gait analysis

The gait cycle runs from one heel strike to the next heel strike of the same foot. In walking, about 60% of the cycle is the stance phase (foot on the ground) and about 40% is the swing phase (foot in the air). Gait analysis, using observation, video, force plates or sensors, looks for asymmetry between the legs, reduced range of motion and poor weight transfer. It is widely used in rehabilitation.

The gait cycle (walking). Analysing each phase helps to find problems and to track recovery after injury.

Limitations and considerations

Technology is only useful if it is used well. Equipment can be expensive and needs training; lab results may not match what happens in competition; errors can come from poor marker placement, low camera frame rates or sensor drift; and large amounts of data are only helpful if they are interpreted correctly. Collecting personal movement and health data also raises privacy and consent issues, so athletes should know how their data is stored and used.

Exam Tips

🎯
Use the four phases in orderFor any skill, name the phases (preparation, force production, critical instant, follow-through or recovery) and say what happens in each, using the sport named.
⚖️
Give both sides for qualitative and quantitativeState what each is, an example, a strength and a limitation. Show that they complement each other. Check the IB command terms.
📈
Quote numbers when you interpret dataFor a force–time graph, refer to body weight, the peak force, the time of flight and what each shows about technique.
🔗
Link the tool to the questionForce plates measure force, video and motion capture measure movement, wearables measure load and EMG measures muscle activity. Match the tool to what needs measuring.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't treat the follow-through as unimportant. It shows how the limb slows down safely and reveals the quality of the earlier phases. Also, the critical instant is a moment, not a whole phase.

1State what is meant by the critical instant of a movement.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • The key moment in a skill, such as ball contact or release, which decides the outcome of the movement.
2Distinguish between qualitative and quantitative movement analysis.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Qualitative analysis is based on observation and descriptive judgement of movement, for example by a coach.
  • Quantitative analysis uses numerical measurement of movement, such as joint angles, speed, time or force.
3Describe the phases of movement for a tennis serve.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Preparation: the ball toss and trophy position, setting up balance and a stretched shoulder and trunk.
  • Force production: the legs drive upwards and the trunk and arm swing the racket up to the ball (with timing and sequence of joints).
  • Critical instant: contact with the ball at full reach. Follow-through: the racket slows down across the body. Any three phases described.
4Explain how a force plate could be used to analyse a vertical jump.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The force plate records the ground reaction force over time, which shows the unweighting, propulsion and landing phases and the peak force.
  • The flight time can be used to calculate jump height (h = g × t² ÷ 8).
  • The impulse (or the difference between the legs) can be used to analyse technique, power or any imbalance, and to track progress.
5Evaluate the use of wearable sensors and motion capture to reduce injury risk in a team sport.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • They provide objective, accurate data, for example on landing mechanics, asymmetry between the legs or accelerations, which can identify movements that increase injury risk.
  • Wearable sensors allow load and movement to be monitored in real training and matches, so coaches can adjust training.
  • Limitations include cost, the need for expertise to interpret data, lab motion capture not matching match conditions, and privacy concerns.
  • They are most useful when combined with coach and physiotherapist observation, as part of an overall injury-prevention programme.
