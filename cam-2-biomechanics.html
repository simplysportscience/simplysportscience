Biomechanics

Linear and angular motion, forces, flight and stability, described with the quantities and equations the specification requires.

Overview

Biomechanics applies the principles of physics to human movement. In this topic you describe how bodies move in a straight or curved line, the forces that cause and change that movement, the way rotating bodies behave, the paths that objects follow when they are projected, and what makes a body stable.

You need to describe, calculate and compare quantities, and draw and interpret graphs. Always state units, and be precise about whether a quantity is a scalar or a vector.

Specification content covered in this topic

Key Terms

Linear motionMovement in a straight or curved line where every part of the body moves the same distance, in the same direction, in the same time.
Scalar quantityA quantity with magnitude only, such as distance or speed.
Vector quantityA quantity with both magnitude and direction, such as displacement or velocity.
MomentumThe product of mass and velocity (p = mv).
Angular momentumThe product of moment of inertia and angular velocity.
Moment of inertiaThe resistance of a body to angular acceleration, which depends on its mass and how that mass is distributed from the axis of rotation.
Centre of massThe point at which the mass of a body is considered to be concentrated.
Base of supportThe area beneath a body that is enclosed by the points of contact with the surface.

Deep Dive

2.1 Linear motion

A body is in linear motion when all its parts move the same distance, in the same direction, over the same time. This is caused by an external force that passes through the centre of mass.

Distance and speed are scalars; displacement and velocity are vectors. Displacement is the straight-line distance from start to finish, with direction. Speed is distance divided by time. Velocity is displacement divided by time. Acceleration is the change in velocity divided by time. Momentum is mass multiplied by velocity.

On a distance–time graph, the gradient gives speed. On a displacement–time graph, the gradient gives velocity. On a velocity–time graph, the gradient gives acceleration, and the area under the line gives displacement. Sketch a graph with labelled axes and units before you interpret it.

2.2 Newton’s laws of motion

Force is a push or pull that can change the motion of a body. Mass is the amount of matter in a body; weight is the gravitational force acting on that mass. Inertia is a body’s resistance to change in motion, and depends on its mass.

The forces acting in physical activity include gravitational force (weight), air resistance, friction, the reaction force from the ground, and the muscular (action) force. Forces can be balanced, producing no change in motion, or unbalanced, producing acceleration.

Newton’s first law (law of inertia): a body stays at rest or in uniform motion unless an unbalanced force acts on it. A sprinter stays still in the blocks until a force from the blocks overcomes their inertia.

Newton’s second law (law of acceleration): acceleration is proportional to the unbalanced force and inversely proportional to mass (F = ma). Increasing the force a sprinter applies to the blocks increases their acceleration.

Newton’s third law (law of reaction): for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. A sprinter pushes backwards on the blocks, and the blocks push forwards on the sprinter with an equal force.

2.3 Angular motion

Angular motion is movement of a body in a circular path about an axis of rotation. It is caused by a force that acts outside the centre of mass, which produces a turning effect.

Three quantities describe it: angular momentum, moment of inertia and angular velocity. They are linked by the equation angular momentum = moment of inertia × angular velocity (L = Iω).

The moment of inertia depends on the mass of the body and the distribution of that mass from the axis of rotation. Mass further from the axis gives a larger moment of inertia.

The conservation of angular momentum means that, with no external torque, angular momentum stays constant. A diver or gymnast who pulls their limbs in reduces their moment of inertia, so their angular velocity increases to keep angular momentum constant. When they extend their body, the moment of inertia rises and the rotation slows. Sketch graphs of all three quantities to show this relationship.

2.4 Parabolic and non-parabolic flight

Once released, a projectile has a horizontal and a vertical component of motion. The horizontal velocity stays roughly constant, while the vertical velocity changes under gravity. Sketch both components when you draw a flight path.

A shot put follows an approximately parabolic flight path, because it is dense and compact, so air resistance has little effect on it. A badminton shuttlecock has a large surface area for its mass, so air resistance has a major effect; its flight is non-parabolic, with a steep descent.

The horizontal displacement of a projectile depends on the height of release, the speed of release and the angle of release. When a projectile lands lower than it was released, the angle that gives maximum horizontal distance is less than 45°, and it falls further as the release height rises.

2.5 Properties of bodies and objects

The centre of mass is the point where the body’s mass is concentrated. Its position depends on the body’s shape and how its mass is distributed, and it moves when the body changes posture.

Stability is greater when the area of the base of support is large, when the centre of mass is low, when the line of gravity falls within the base of support, and when the mass of the body is greater. A wide stance in a rugby scrum and a low crouch in a goalkeeper’s stance both apply these principles.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1A 90 kg player is moving at 6 m/s. Calculate the momentum of the player.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Momentum = mass × velocity = 90 kg × 6 m/s.
  • = 540 kg m/s.
2Explain why a diver rotates faster when they tuck than when they extend their body.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Tucking brings the mass closer to the axis of rotation, which reduces the moment of inertia.
  • Angular momentum is conserved, so when the moment of inertia falls the angular velocity increases.
  • Extending the body does the reverse, slowing the rotation.
3Explain why the flight path of a shot put is parabolic, but the flight path of a badminton shuttlecock is not.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The shot put is dense and compact, so air resistance has little effect on its flight.
  • The shuttlecock has a large surface area for its mass, so air resistance is significant and changes its path.
  • Its descent is therefore steeper and not parabolic.
4Explain two factors that make a rugby player less likely to be knocked over.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • A wider base of support, because the line of gravity is more likely to fall within it.
  • A lower centre of mass, which makes the body more stable.
5A sprinter pushes backwards against the blocks. Using Newton’s third law, explain how this produces forward motion.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.
  • The blocks push the sprinter forwards with a force equal to the force the sprinter applies backwards.
