B.1.3 Theme B · Generating Movement in the Body SL & HL

Muscular Function

How the nervous system recruits muscle, how different fibre types and contraction types suit different tasks, and how muscles work in teams to move the body.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Skeletal muscles generate the force that moves the bones. Each muscle is controlled in motor units, and different muscle fibre types are suited to different jobs, from marathon running to sprinting. Muscles can produce force while shortening (concentric), lengthening (eccentric) or staying the same length (isometric). They also work in teams, with an agonist producing the movement, an antagonist controlling it, and other muscles assisting and stabilising. At higher level you also study the sliding filament theory, which explains how a muscle shortens at the molecular level.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Describe how a motor neuron activates a muscle, and define a motor unit.
  • Compare type I, type IIa and type IIx muscle fibres and explain how they are recruited.
  • Describe isometric, concentric, eccentric and isokinetic contractions with sporting examples.
  • Identify agonist, antagonist, synergist and fixator roles, and explain reciprocal inhibition.
  • HL: Explain the sliding filament theory, including the roles of calcium, ATP, actin, myosin, troponin and tropomyosin.

Key Terms

Motor unitA motor neuron and all of the muscle fibres it supplies.
Concentric contractionThe muscle shortens while producing force.
Eccentric contractionThe muscle lengthens while producing force, usually to control a movement.
AgonistThe main muscle responsible for producing a movement (the prime mover).
Reciprocal inhibitionWhen the agonist contracts, the antagonist is signalled to relax so the movement is smooth.
Sarcomere (HL)The basic contractile unit of a muscle fibre, from one Z-disc to the next.

Deep Dive

Motor units and recruitment

A muscle contracts when a nerve impulse travels along a motor neuron and crosses the neuromuscular junction, where acetylcholine triggers an action potential in the muscle fibres (see A.1.1). A motor unit is one motor neuron and every muscle fibre it supplies. When a motor neuron fires, all its fibres contract together (the all-or-none principle). Muscles that need fine control, such as those moving the fingers or eye, have small motor units with only a few fibres each. Muscles that need power, such as the quadriceps, have large motor units with hundreds or thousands of fibres.

The body controls force in two ways: by recruiting more motor units, and by firing each unit more often. Motor units are normally recruited from smallest to largest (the size principle), so low-force tasks use small, slow units first, and large, fast units are only added when more force or speed is needed.

The proportion of each fibre type differs between people. It is strongly influenced by genetics, so elite endurance athletes tend to have more type I fibres in their leg muscles, and elite sprinters have more type II. Training can improve the fatigue resistance of type II fibres (for example, type IIx fibres take on type IIa features with endurance training), but it cannot change a person's fibre-type mix very much.

Recruitment by effort, following the size principle. Type I fibres are used at all effort levels; type IIa and IIx fibres are added as the demand for force rises. Percentages are approximate.

Concentric, eccentric and isometric contractions in a biceps curl. In all three the muscle is producing force: what differs is whether its length changes.

Eccentric contractions can produce more force than concentric ones and use less energy, but they cause more muscle damage, which is the main cause of delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS). In many sporting movements an eccentric contraction is followed straight away by a concentric one (for example in plyometric jumps), which gives extra power from the stored elastic energy.

How muscles work together

  • Agonist (prime mover): the main muscle producing the movement.
  • Antagonist: the muscle that opposes the agonist. It relaxes to allow the movement, and often works eccentrically to slow the limb down at the end.
  • Synergist: a muscle that helps the agonist.
  • Fixator (stabiliser): a muscle that holds a joint or bone steady so the agonist has a firm base to pull from.

The nervous system coordinates this through reciprocal inhibition: when the agonist is signalled to contract, the antagonist is signalled to relax, so the movement is smooth and efficient. For control and safety, the antagonist is often active as well, braking the movement.

Sliding filament theory HL

A muscle fibre is packed with myofibrils, which are made of repeating units called sarcomeres. Each sarcomere contains thin filaments (actin, with the proteins troponin and tropomyosin) and thick filaments (myosin). In the sliding filament theory, the filaments slide past each other without changing length, which shortens the sarcomere and so the whole muscle.

A sarcomere relaxed and contracted. The thin filaments slide over the thick filaments, pulling the Z-discs closer together.

The process, step by step:

  • A nerve impulse reaches the muscle fibre and an action potential spreads along it, causing calcium ions (Ca²⁺) to be released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
  • Calcium binds to troponin, which makes tropomyosin move and expose the binding sites on actin.
  • A myosin head, already holding ADP and phosphate, binds to actin and forms a cross-bridge.
  • The head pivots (the power stroke), pulling the actin filament towards the centre of the sarcomere, and ADP and phosphate are released.
  • A new ATP molecule binds to myosin, which makes the head detach from actin. The ATP is split, re-cocking the head so that it can bind again.
  • The cycle repeats while calcium and ATP are available. When the nerve signal stops, calcium is actively pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum, tropomyosin blocks the binding sites again, and the muscle relaxes.
The cross-bridge cycle. ATP is needed both for the power stroke cycle (to detach and re-cock the head) and to pump calcium back after contraction.

Without ATP, myosin heads cannot detach from actin, which is why muscles become stiff after death (rigor mortis). During exercise, running short of ATP, or an increase in the by-products of ATP use, contributes to fatigue (see A.3.3).

Exam Tips

🎯
Anchor each contraction type to lengthAlways state what happens to muscle length: concentric shortens, eccentric lengthens under tension, isometric stays the same, with force produced in all three.
🤝
Name the agonist and the antagonistGive both for a joint action, such as "knee extension: quadriceps (agonist), hamstrings (antagonist)", and mention reciprocal inhibition if asked about control.
🏃
Link fibre type to the sportName the type, its feature (speed, force, fatigue resistance) and the activity it suits. Check the IB command terms.
🧬
For sliding filament (HL), keep the sequenceCalcium → troponin → tropomyosin moves → myosin binds actin → power stroke → ATP binds → detach. Name the job of each molecule.
⚠️
Common mistakeEccentric does not mean the muscle is relaxed or switched off. It lengthens while producing force. Likewise, the antagonist is not simply "off": it is often working eccentrically to control the movement.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1Define motor unit.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • A motor neuron and all of the muscle fibres that it supplies (innervates).
2Distinguish between concentric and eccentric muscle contractions.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • In a concentric contraction the muscle shortens while producing force, causing or accelerating movement.
  • In an eccentric contraction the muscle lengthens while producing force, controlling or slowing the movement.
3Outline three differences between type I and type IIx muscle fibres.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Type I fibres contract slowly, whereas type IIx fibres contract fast.
  • Type I fibres produce lower force, whereas type IIx fibres produce high force.
  • Type I fibres are highly resistant to fatigue, whereas type IIx fibres fatigue quickly.
  • Type I fibres have more mitochondria, capillaries and myoglobin, and rely on the oxidative system; type IIx fibres rely on anaerobic energy systems. Any three.
4Identify the agonist and antagonist during knee extension in a football kick, and explain the role of reciprocal inhibition.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The agonist is the quadriceps and the antagonist is the hamstrings.
  • Reciprocal inhibition is when the nervous system signals the antagonist to relax as the agonist contracts.
  • This allows the agonist to move the joint smoothly and efficiently without working against the antagonist.
5(HL) Explain the roles of calcium and ATP in the sliding filament theory.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Calcium ions are released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum and bind to troponin, which moves tropomyosin away and exposes the binding sites on actin.
  • The myosin heads can then bind to actin, forming cross-bridges.
  • The energy from splitting ATP is used to re-cock the myosin head (and powers the power stroke), which pulls the actin filaments towards the centre of the sarcomere.
  • A new ATP molecule must bind to myosin for the head to detach from actin, so that the cycle can repeat.
