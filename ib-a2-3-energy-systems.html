A.2.3 Theme A · Hydration & Nutrition SL & HL

Energy Systems

How the body makes ATP to power movement, how the three energy systems share the work from a sprint to a marathon, and what limits and follows aerobic exercise.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Every muscle contraction is powered by one molecule: ATP. Muscles store only a tiny amount, so ATP must be made again, continuously, by three energy systems. The phosphagen system is fastest but runs out in seconds. The glycolytic system supports hard efforts for up to a couple of minutes. The oxidative system uses oxygen and can keep going for hours. All three work at the same time, and the one that dominates depends on how intense and how long the activity is. At higher level you also study the lactate inflection point and excess post-exercise oxygen consumption (EPOC).

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Explain how ATP provides energy and how it is resynthesised.
  • Describe the phosphagen, glycolytic and oxidative systems, including fuel, oxygen use, rate, capacity and by-products.
  • Explain the energy continuum and apply it to different sports.
  • Explain the factors that affect maximal oxygen consumption (VO₂ max).
  • HL: Explain the lactate inflection point and its significance.
  • HL: Explain excess post-exercise oxygen consumption (EPOC) and its role in recovery.

Deep Dive

ATP: the energy currency

When the bond holding the end phosphate of ATP is broken, ATP becomes ADP (adenosine diphosphate) plus a phosphate, and energy is released for muscle contraction. Muscles hold only enough ATP for a couple of seconds of maximal effort, so ADP must be rapidly turned back into ATP. This needs energy, which comes from the breakdown of fuels (phosphocreatine, carbohydrate, fat and protein) by the three energy systems.

ATP releases energy when it is broken down to ADP and phosphate. The three energy systems use the energy in different fuels to join them back together again.

The key trade-off is rate versus capacity. The systems that make ATP fastest can only make a little, and the system that can make a huge amount is slower. Notice also that lactate is not simply a waste product: it can be used as a fuel by the heart and by other muscle fibres. It is the accumulation of hydrogen ions, which lowers muscle pH, that is linked to fatigue in hard exercise.

The energy continuum

The three systems do not switch on and off like lights. All three contribute at all times, but their share changes. The more intense the activity, the faster ATP is needed, so the phosphagen and glycolytic systems provide more. The longer the activity, the greater the share from the oxidative system. A sprinter's 10 seconds is mostly phosphagen, a 400 m is largely glycolytic, and a marathon is almost entirely oxidative.

Approximate contribution of each energy system to maximal efforts of different durations. Values are estimates and vary between sources and individuals; the pattern is what matters.

Many sports are intermittent. A football player spends most of a match jogging and walking (oxidative), with repeated sprints that draw on the phosphagen and glycolytic systems. A strong oxidative system also helps players recover between sprints, because it restores phosphocreatine.

Maximal oxygen consumption (VO₂ max)

VO₂ max is the maximum volume of oxygen the body can take in, transport and use per minute during exercise. It is usually given relative to body mass in mL per kg per minute, and it is the best single measure of aerobic fitness. Typical values are around 35–45 for untrained young adults, and 70–85 or more for elite endurance athletes (women typically about 10–15% lower than men).

FactorHow it affects VO₂ max
Cardiac outputStroke volume and heart rate set how much oxygen-rich blood is delivered to the muscles (see A.1.3). This is the main limiter in most people.
Oxygen-carrying capacityMore haemoglobin and blood volume means more oxygen carried per minute.
Muscle oxygen useMore capillaries and mitochondria, and more oxidative enzymes, mean the muscle can extract and use more oxygen.
Muscle fibre typeSlow-twitch fibres are better equipped for oxygen use than fast-twitch fibres.
Training statusEndurance training raises VO₂ max, usually by about 10–20% in previously untrained people.
GeneticsHeredity strongly influences both starting level and trainability.
Age and sexVO₂ max falls with age after the mid-20s. Males tend to be higher, partly through larger hearts, greater blood volume and higher haemoglobin.
Body compositionMore body fat lowers VO₂ max expressed per kg of body mass.
AltitudeLess oxygen in the air lowers VO₂ max.
Mode of exerciseUsing more muscle mass (running) gives a higher VO₂ max than using less (arm cranking).

Lactate inflection point HL

The body produces lactate all the time, even at rest, and also removes and uses it. At low intensities, production and removal are balanced, so blood lactate stays low and steady. The lactate inflection point (LIP) is the intensity at which blood lactate begins to rise rapidly, because production now exceeds removal. Beyond this point, hydrogen ions build up and the athlete tires quickly.

Blood lactate against running speed. Training moves the lactate inflection point to the right, so the athlete can run faster before lactate accumulates.

The LIP matters for endurance athletes because it indicates the highest pace that can be sustained for a long time. Endurance training shifts the LIP to a higher speed or a higher percentage of VO₂ max, because trained muscles make less lactate and clear more of it. Coaches use the LIP to set training zones and race pacing, for example by training just below or at it to raise it further.

Excess post-exercise oxygen consumption (EPOC) HL

At the start of exercise, oxygen uptake takes a minute or two to rise to the level needed. During this time ATP is supplied partly by the anaerobic systems, creating an oxygen deficit. After exercise, oxygen uptake does not fall straight back to the resting level. It stays elevated, and the extra oxygen consumed above the resting level is the excess post-exercise oxygen consumption (EPOC).

Oxygen uptake before, during and after steady exercise. The shaded areas show the oxygen deficit at the start and the EPOC during recovery.
  • Fast component (the first few minutes): restoring ATP and phosphocreatine stores, replenishing oxygen stores in the blood and muscle (myoglobin), and a heart rate and breathing rate that are still raised.
  • Slow component (can last for a long time after hard exercise): clearing lactate, a body temperature that is still raised, elevated hormones such as adrenaline, and tissue repair.

EPOC is larger after high-intensity and long exercise, because more of the body's stores have been used and more heat and lactate produced. It shows why the body needs time to recover between hard efforts: a footballer who sprints repeatedly builds up a large EPOC, which is why breathing stays heavy after the sprint is over.

Exam Tips

🎯
Say "dominant", not "only"All three systems contribute all the time. Write "the phosphagen system is the main contributor in the first 10 seconds", never "only the ATP-PC system is used".
🔬
Justify the system with intensity and durationName the system, then give the reason: fuel, oxygen, rate of ATP supply and how long it lasts. Linking the sport to the continuum earns the application mark.
📏
Use correct units and termsVO₂ max is in mL/kg/min (relative) or L/min (absolute). Check the IB command terms to see whether you need to "outline" or "explain".
📈
Describe graphs in order (HL)For LIP and EPOC graphs, state the baseline, where the curve changes, and the direction and size of the change, then explain why using production and removal of lactate, or the fast and slow components.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't say "lactic acid causes muscle soreness and fatigue". Lactate is a useful fuel and is not the cause of next-day soreness. The build-up of hydrogen ions, which lowers muscle pH, is linked to fatigue in hard exercise.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1State the main fuel used by the phosphagen system.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • Phosphocreatine (PC) stored in the muscle (together with the small store of ATP).
2Distinguish between the glycolytic and oxidative energy systems.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The glycolytic system breaks down glucose or glycogen without oxygen, producing a small amount of ATP and lactate with hydrogen ions.
  • The oxidative system uses oxygen to break down carbohydrate and fat, producing a large amount of ATP with carbon dioxide and water as by-products.
3Outline three factors that influence an athlete's VO₂ max.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Any three of: genetics/heredity; training status (endurance training raises it); age (declines with age); sex (usually higher in males); body composition (more body fat lowers it per kg); altitude (less oxygen available).
  • Or physiological factors: stroke volume and cardiac output; haemoglobin and blood volume; capillary density and mitochondria in the muscle.
4(HL) Explain what is meant by the lactate inflection point and why a higher value benefits an endurance athlete.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The lactate inflection point is the exercise intensity at which blood lactate begins to rise rapidly, because lactate production exceeds removal.
  • Below this intensity lactate production and removal are balanced, so the athlete can keep working at a steady state for a long time.
  • A higher inflection point means the athlete can sustain a faster pace or higher percentage of VO₂ max before lactate and hydrogen ions accumulate and cause fatigue.
5Explain how the energy continuum applies to a 400 m sprint.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • All three energy systems contribute at all times, but the dominant system changes with the intensity and duration of the race.
  • At the start, the phosphagen system supplies ATP most rapidly (using phosphocreatine) for the first few seconds, until PC runs low.
  • As the race continues at high intensity, the glycolytic system becomes the main contributor, producing ATP quickly without oxygen but also lactate and hydrogen ions.
  • The oxidative system provides an increasing share as the race goes on, but it supplies ATP too slowly to be dominant in a 45–60 second effort.
