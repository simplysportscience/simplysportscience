A.3.3 Theme A · Response HL only

Fatigue & Recovery

Why performance falls during exercise, how to tell when an athlete has recovered, and which nutrition, sleep and recovery techniques work best, and when.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

This whole topic is Higher Level only. Fatigue is a reduction in the ability to produce force or keep up performance. It can start in the brain and nervous system (central fatigue) or in the working muscles (peripheral fatigue), and it is influenced by fuel, hydration, electrolytes and energy availability. Recovery is the process of restoring the body and mind after exercise. Good recovery depends on matching the strategy (nutrition, sleep and techniques such as cooling or compression) to the cause of the fatigue, the time available and the individual athlete.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Distinguish between central and peripheral fatigue, and explain their neuromuscular and metabolic causes.
  • Explain how hydration, electrolytes and energy availability influence fatigue.
  • Describe recovery processes and timescales, and classify indicators of recovery.
  • Outline hydration and nutritional strategies for recovery.
  • Evaluate recovery techniques and the factors that influence recovery quality, including sleep.

Key Terms

FatigueA reduction in the ability to produce force or maintain performance.
Central fatigueFatigue from the nervous system, with less neural drive to the muscles.
Peripheral fatigueFatigue within or near the working muscles, reducing force or speed.
RecoveryRestoring the body and mind after exercise so performance can return.
Active recoveryLow-intensity exercise after hard exercise, such as a cool-down jog or easy cycling.
DOMSDelayed onset muscle soreness: aching that peaks 24–72 hours after unfamiliar or hard exercise.

Deep Dive

Where does fatigue come from?

For a muscle to contract, a signal has to travel from the brain, down the spinal cord and motor neuron, across the neuromuscular junction, through the muscle fibre membrane, and then the muscle needs energy to run the cross-bridges. Fatigue can arise at any point along this chain.

Where central and peripheral fatigue occur along the pathway from brain to muscle, and some possible causes. These are contributors; several often act together.
Central fatiguePeripheral fatigue
SiteBrain and spinal cord (nervous system)Neuromuscular junction and muscle
What goes wrongReduced neural drive and voluntary activation, lower motivation, higher perceived effortThe muscle produces less force or speed, even when the drive is high
ExamplesLate in an ultramarathon in the heat; a long, monotonous sessionThe last 30 m of a 100 m sprint (PC depletion, H⁺); the end of a marathon (glycogen)

Metabolic causes, fuel, hydration and energy availability

  • Fuel: in very short, maximal efforts phosphocreatine runs down. In long events, falling muscle and liver glycogen means ATP cannot be made fast enough, and low blood glucose also affects the brain (central fatigue).
  • Metabolic by-products: hydrogen ions (lowering pH), phosphate and ADP accumulate in intense exercise, impairing muscle contraction.
  • Hydration and electrolytes: dehydration reduces blood volume and stroke volume, raises heart rate and core temperature, and makes the effort feel harder. Sodium and potassium imbalances impair nerve and muscle function (see A.2.1).
  • Energy availability: when energy intake is persistently too low for the training load, fatigue becomes chronic and recovery is poor (see A.2.2).
  • Heat: a raised core temperature increases the sense of effort and reduces neural drive.

Recovery processes and timescales

Different systems recover at different speeds. Phosphocreatine is restored in a few minutes, so short rests between sprints help repeat performance. Glycogen and tissue repair take much longer, so they need days of planning during a tournament or heavy training block.

Indicators of recovery

Strong answers classify the signs of recovery into three types:

TypeWhat it showsExamples
PhysiologicalMeasurable body changesHeart rate and breathing back to resting; blood lactate returned to resting level; body temperature and hydration normal (urine colour, body mass); resting heart rate and heart rate variability; performance tests such as a jump height
SymptomaticWhat the athlete feels in the bodyMuscle soreness and stiffness (DOMS), general tiredness, sleep quality, appetite
PsychologicalMood and readinessMotivation, mood, concentration and feeling ready to train, often tracked with simple daily wellness questionnaires

If an athlete's performance, mood and sleep keep getting worse and a raised resting heart rate or frequent illness appear despite rest, they may be overreaching or have overtraining syndrome, and the training load must be reduced.

Hydration and nutritional strategies for recovery

StrategyPurpose and typical guidance
Fluids and sodiumReplace sweat losses: drink about 125–150% of the body mass lost over the next hours, with sodium from food or drinks, because urine production continues.
CarbohydrateRestore glycogen. When there is less than about 8 hours before the next session, aim for roughly 1.0–1.2 g per kg of body mass per hour for the first 4 hours. If there is plenty of time, normal meals are enough.
ProteinProvides amino acids to repair and rebuild muscle. About 0.25–0.3 g per kg (roughly 20–25 g) in each meal, spread across the day. Combined with carbohydrate it can help refuelling when carbohydrate intake is low.
Overall energyEating enough total energy supports every recovery process (see A.2.2).

A strategy is only useful if it matches the demands of the event, the cause of fatigue, the time available and what the athlete can tolerate. A day-before-match recovery shake is helpful; the same plan after an easy session is probably unnecessary.

Matching the main cause of fatigue to a suitable recovery strategy. This is a guide, not a rule: the right choice depends on the athlete and the schedule.

Recovery techniques

  • Active recovery and cool-down: light exercise keeps blood flowing and helps clear lactate faster than complete rest, and is a practical routine. It does not greatly speed up tissue repair.
  • Cold-water immersion: typically 10–15 minutes in water at about 10–15 °C. It can reduce soreness and perceived fatigue in the short term and lower core temperature after hot exercise. Repeated use straight after strength training may reduce muscle growth adaptations.
  • Thermotherapy (heat or cold methods): heat such as a hot bath or sauna increases blood flow and promotes relaxation; contrast therapy alternates hot and cold. Evidence for performance benefits is limited.
  • Compression garments: may modestly reduce soreness and perceived fatigue, with little clear effect on performance.
  • Massage and foam rolling: reduce soreness and improve how recovered an athlete feels, but evidence of faster physiological recovery is small.
  • Sleep: the most important recovery tool. Growth hormone is released and tissue repair, learning and immune function all benefit. Most adults need about 7–9 hours, and athletes in heavy training often need more. Poor sleep impairs reaction time, decision-making, mood and recovery.

Factors that influence recovery quality

Recovery is individual. Factors include the type, intensity and length of exercise (unfamiliar or eccentric work causes more muscle damage), age and training status, nutrition and hydration, sleep, psychological stress, the environment (heat and altitude) and the schedule, including travel across time zones. A good recovery plan is chosen using these factors and then reviewed, using the indicators above.

Exam Tips

🎯
Name the site of the fatigueSay "central: reduced neural drive from the brain" or "peripheral: phosphocreatine depleted in the muscle". Name the cause and the place.
🗂️
Classify the indicatorsFor recovery indicators, sort them into physiological, symptomatic and psychological, and give a measurable example of each.
⚖️
Evaluate with balanceFor "evaluate" or "discuss", give benefits and limits, then a conclusion that depends on the athlete, event and time available. Check the IB command terms.
🔗
Match the strategy to the causeGlycogen depletion needs carbohydrate, dehydration needs fluid and sodium, and central fatigue needs sleep and reduced load. Show you can choose, not just list.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't assume fatigue is only "the muscles running out of energy". Central fatigue matters too, especially in long, hot events. And don't claim that lactate causes next-day soreness: lactate is cleared within about an hour, and DOMS is linked to muscle damage and inflammation.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1State one cause of peripheral fatigue during a 100 m sprint.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • Depletion of phosphocreatine in the muscle, or accumulation of hydrogen ions, phosphate and ADP in the muscle (any one).
2Distinguish between central and peripheral fatigue.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Central fatigue occurs in the nervous system (brain and spinal cord), when there is reduced neural drive or voluntary activation to the muscles.
  • Peripheral fatigue occurs at or within the muscle (including the neuromuscular junction), when the muscle produces less force even if the drive is high.
3Outline three indicators of recovery, using the categories physiological, symptomatic and psychological.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Physiological: for example heart rate returning to its resting level, or blood lactate returning to normal.
  • Symptomatic: for example reduced muscle soreness or improved sleep quality.
  • Psychological: for example improved mood, motivation or feeling ready to train.
4Describe the hydration and nutritional strategies an athlete should use in the hours after a hard training session when they have a further session the next morning.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Drink about 125–150% of the body mass lost as fluid, together with sodium, to restore hydration.
  • Eat carbohydrate soon after exercise and in the following hours (about 1.0–1.2 g per kg per hour for the first 4 hours) to restore muscle glycogen.
  • Include protein (about 20–25 g) in meals to supply amino acids for muscle repair.
5Evaluate the use of cold-water immersion as a recovery technique.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Benefit: it can reduce muscle soreness and perceived fatigue in the short term.
  • Benefit: it can lower core temperature quickly after hot exercise, which helps when there is a short time between events.
  • Limitation: evidence that it speeds up physiological recovery or improves later performance is modest, and regular use after strength training may reduce muscle adaptations.
  • Conclusion: it is most useful for short-term recovery between competitions or in hot conditions, and less suitable during a training block aimed at building strength; the decision depends on the athlete and the schedule.
