C.3.1 Theme C · Motivation SL & HL

Achievement Motivation

Why athletes approach or avoid challenges: how personality and the situation combine, what drives task choice and persistence, and how coaches can change the balance.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Achievement motivation explains why people approach, persist in or avoid achievement situations, such as competitions, tests and difficult training. Atkinson's model proposes that this depends on two personality tendencies, the motive to achieve success and the motive to avoid failure, and on situational factors such as the probability of success and the incentive value of success. The balance between the approach and avoidance tendencies predicts how an athlete will feel and behave. Coaches can influence this balance by changing the situation, for example by setting tasks at a suitable level and focusing on personal progress.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Explain achievement motivation as the balance between approaching success and avoiding failure.
  • Identify the personality and situational factors that combine in Atkinson's model.
  • Explain the chain from personality and situation to resultant tendency, emotion and behaviour.
  • Explain how achievement motivation affects task choice and persistence.
  • Explain task orientation, and why task-oriented performers are more likely to persist after failure.
  • Apply coaching strategies that increase approach motivation and reduce avoidance motivation.

Key Terms

Achievement motivationThe psychological processes that drive a person to approach, persist in or avoid achievement situations.
Motive to achieve success (Ms)The personality tendency to seek success and the pride that comes with it.
Motive to avoid failure (Maf)The personality tendency to avoid failure and the fear that comes with it.
Probability of success (Ps)The likelihood of succeeding at a task. Probability of failure (Pf) = 1 − Ps.
Resultant achievement motivationThe balance of approach and avoidance tendencies for a particular task.
Task orientationJudging success by self-referenced progress, effort, learning and mastery.

Deep Dive

The personality and situation chain

Achievement motivation can be followed as a chain. The personality factors are the performer's tendency to seek achievement (Ms) and to avoid failure (Maf). The situational factors include the probability of success, the difficulty of the task, the importance of the outcome and the feedback given by others. Together they produce a resultant tendency: approach if the motive to achieve success is stronger, avoidance if the motive to avoid failure is stronger. This tendency then produces an emotion (pride and confidence, or fear and anxiety) and behaviour (choosing, persisting with or avoiding the task).

The chain from personality and situation to resultant tendency, emotion and behaviour, with a gymnastics example. The same situation can lead to very different responses in performers with different motives.

Atkinson's model

In Atkinson's model, the resultant tendency is often written as:

Achievement motivation = Ms − Maf

The motive to achieve success is the product of the probability of success and the incentive value of success (Ms = Ps × Is). The motive to avoid failure depends on fear of failure, the probability of failure and the negative incentive value of failure. Because Ps + Pf = 1, a task that is easy (high Ps) carries little incentive for a performer who wants to succeed, and a task that is very hard (low Ps) carries little risk of a personal failure. This is why the two motives lead to different task choices.

Task choice and persistence

The model predicts that performers with a strong motive to achieve success prefer tasks of moderate difficulty, where success is realistic but not certain, and that they persist longer in these tasks. Performers with a strong fear of failure prefer very easy or very hard tasks: very easy tasks protect against failure, and very hard tasks give a ready explanation for failing. This pattern is shown in the schematic curves below. The model is a simplified prediction; real preferences vary between individuals and situations.

Schematic task-choice preferences predicted by Atkinson's model. Approach-oriented performers prefer moderate difficulty; avoidance-oriented performers prefer very easy or very difficult tasks.

In sport, this helps explain behaviour such as a player who avoids taking responsibility in a tense match, or an athlete who only enters races they are sure to win. Recognising the pattern lets a coach adjust the task instead of simply pushing the athlete harder.

Task orientation

Performers also differ in how they define success. A task-oriented performer judges success by personal progress, skill development, effort and learning. An ego-oriented performer judges success by outperforming others. Task-oriented performers are more likely to persist after failure, because they interpret failure as information for improvement rather than as a judgement of their ability. This links to attribution and locus of control (see C.1.2).

Coaching strategies

Because achievement motivation depends on the situation as well as personality, coaches can shift the balance towards approach and away from avoidance by changing the environment:

  • Raise the probability of success: start with tasks the athlete can succeed at, and build the difficulty gradually.
  • Make success meaningful: set personal targets and recognise progress, not only the final result.
  • Reduce the cost of failure: treat errors as information, and avoid public blame or criticism.
  • Build a task-focused climate: reward effort, learning and improvement, rather than only comparison with others.
Four coaching strategies for changing the situation so that approach motivation increases and avoidance motivation falls. Examples are illustrative.

Evaluating the model

Atkinson's model has been useful for explaining task choice, but it has limitations. It treats motives as fixed personality tendencies, although they can change with experience and coaching. The measures of motives and of the incentive value of success are hard to define and to test in real sport. The model also focuses on individual motivation, and does not fully capture team and social influences, or the role of goals and the coach. It is best used as one tool among several, to help understand and guide behaviour rather than to predict it exactly.

Exam Tips

🎯
Use the chain for "explain" questionsPersonality (Ms and Maf) and situation (Ps and incentive) → resultant tendency → emotion → behaviour. Apply each step to the sporting example. Check the IB command terms.
📐
Show the balanceWrite Ms − Maf to show that achievement motivation depends on both motives. Say which is stronger for the athlete in the question.
🎚️
Link difficulty to task choiceHigh Ms: moderate difficulty and persistence. High Maf: very easy or very hard tasks, and withdrawal from challenge. Give the reason for each.
🧭
Apply the coaching strategiesFor coaching questions, name the strategy (for example graded difficulty) and explain how it changes Ps or the cost of failure.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't say that high achievers always take the hardest challenges. Atkinson's model predicts moderate difficulty for high Ms. Also, don't describe motivation as a fixed trait that cannot change.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1Define achievement motivation.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • The psychological processes that drive a person to approach, persist in or avoid achievement situations in sport, exercise and health.
2Identify two situational factors that affect achievement motivation.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Probability of success (or difficulty of the task).
  • Importance or incentive value of the outcome, or feedback from others (any two).
3Explain why an athlete with a high motive to achieve success might choose a task of moderate difficulty.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • A moderate task gives a realistic but uncertain chance of success, which makes success meaningful and rewarding.
  • This maximises the incentive value of success for the athlete, so their motive to achieve success is most engaged.
  • The athlete can also feel that success is due to their own ability and effort, which supports pride and persistence.
4Explain how a coach could use the situation to increase approach motivation in an athlete who fears failure.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Start with tasks where success is likely (high probability of success), and build difficulty gradually.
  • Focus feedback on personal progress and learning, and avoid public criticism, so that the cost of failure is reduced.
  • These changes shift the balance of Ms and Maf towards approach over time.
5(HL) Discuss the view that achievement motivation depends on both personality and the situation.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Personality factors (Ms and Maf) determine the performer's tendency to approach or avoid achievement situations.
  • Situational factors, such as the probability of success, task difficulty and feedback from others, change how strongly those tendencies are expressed in a given task.
  • For example, a high-Maf athlete may approach a task when the probability of success is high and the cost of failure is low, which shows the interaction.
  • Limitations: the model treats motives as fixed and is hard to test in real sport, so it should be used as a guide rather than an exact prediction.
