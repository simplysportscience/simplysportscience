The Cardiovascular System

Heart structure, the conduction system, cardiac output and how the circulation redistributes blood during exercise.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

The cardiovascular system transports oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, hormones and heat around the body. The heart is a double pump: the right side sends blood to the lungs, and the left side sends blood to the rest of the body. Understanding its structure, its electrical control, and how it responds to exercise is central to this topic.

You need to know typical values for heart rate, stroke volume and cardiac output at rest and during exercise for both an untrained individual and a trained athlete. These values are given in the specification and examiners expect you to use them.

Key Terms

Cardiac outputThe volume of blood pumped by the heart each minute (heart rate × stroke volume).
Stroke volumeThe volume of blood ejected from the left ventricle in one contraction.
Sinoatrial node (SAN)The heart’s natural pacemaker, in the right atrium, which initiates each heartbeat.
Atrioventricular node (AVN)The node that delays the impulse before it passes to the ventricles.
Venous returnThe flow of blood back to the heart through the veins.
Vascular shuntThe redistribution of blood flow, away from inactive tissues and towards working muscles, during exercise.

Deep Dive

3.1 Structure and function of the heart

The heart has four chambers. The atria receive blood and the ventricles pump it out. The septum separates the left and right sides. The myocardium is the muscular wall, and it is thickest in the left ventricle, which pumps blood around the whole body.

Four valves prevent backflow. The bicuspid (mitral) valve sits between the left atrium and left ventricle; the tricuspid valve between the right atrium and right ventricle; and the aortic and pulmonary semilunar valves guard the exits of the two ventricles.

Follow the pathway of blood through the heart: the vena cava brings deoxygenated blood into the right atrium; it passes through the tricuspid valve to the right ventricle, then through the pulmonary semilunar valve into the pulmonary artery to the lungs. Oxygenated blood returns through the pulmonary vein to the left atrium, through the bicuspid valve to the left ventricle, and out through the aortic valve into the aorta.

3.2 The conduction system and the cardiac cycle

The impulse starts in the sinoatrial node (SAN) in the right atrium. The wave of excitation spreads across both atria, causing atrial systole (contraction). The impulse reaches the atrioventricular node (AVN), which delays it briefly, so that the atria finish emptying before the ventricles contract.

The impulse then passes down the bundle of His and along the Purkyne tissue, which spreads it through the ventricle walls, causing ventricular systole to begin at the base of the heart and move upwards. Between beats the heart is in diastole, when the chambers fill.

The link between conduction and the cycle is timing: the SAN sets the rate, the wave spreads through the atria, the AVN introduces the delay, and the Purkyne tissue triggers ventricular contraction.

3.3 Factors affecting cardiac output

Cardiac output is heart rate multiplied by stroke volume: cardiac output (l/min) = heart rate (beats/min) × stroke volume (l). Use this equation to calculate any one value if you know the other two, and remember to convert millilitres to litres.

Starling’s law and the control of heart rate

Starling’s law states that the more the ventricle is stretched by venous return, the greater the force of its contraction and the larger the stroke volume. Exercise increases venous return, so the heart fills more and ejects more with each beat.

Heart rate is controlled by the cardiac control centre in the medulla. Neural control comes from baroreceptors (blood pressure), chemoreceptors (blood CO₂ and pH) and mechanoreceptors (movement of limbs and joints). The sympathetic nervous system speeds the heart up, and the parasympathetic system slows it. Hormonal control is by adrenaline, which increases heart rate and contractility. Intrinsic control is by stretch of the myocardium and by temperature.

Responses to exercise

At rest, heart rate and stroke volume are low and parasympathetic activity dominates. Immediately before exercise, heart rate rises even before movement starts, because of adrenaline and the sympathetic nervous system: this is the anticipatory rise.

During exercise, heart rate rises with intensity, and stroke volume rises up to a point and then levels off. Cardiac output therefore increases as intensity rises. During recovery, heart rate falls quickly at first, then more slowly, as parasympathetic activity returns.

Sketch heart rate, stroke volume and cardiac output against time on the same axis, and label rest, pre-exercise, exercise and recovery. Use the typical values from the table to show scale.

Long-term training

Regular endurance training produces physiological changes in the heart. A trained athlete has a larger stroke volume at rest and during exercise, so they can achieve the same cardiac output with a lower heart rate. This gives a lower resting heart rate and a higher maximum stroke volume.

3.4 Function of the vascular system

Venous return depends on several mechanisms: the skeletal muscle pump squeezes veins as muscles contract; valves prevent backflow; the respiratory pump uses pressure changes during breathing; gravity works against return from the legs; smooth muscle (venous tone) narrows the veins; and cardiac suction draws blood in during diastole.

During exercise, cardiac output is redistributed by the vascular shunt mechanism. The sympathetic nervous system causes vasoconstriction in arterioles supplying inactive tissue, such as the gut and kidneys, while vasodilation in the arterioles of working muscles increases their blood flow. Precapillary sphincters control flow into capillary beds, and the vasomotor control centre coordinates the response, using information from receptors.

During recovery, blood flow returns to the tissues that were inactive, and the skeletal muscle pump continues to help venous return.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1Calculate the cardiac output of a person with a heart rate of 150 beats per minute and a stroke volume of 120 ml.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Stroke volume = 120 ml = 0.12 l.
  • Cardiac output = 150 × 0.12 = 18 l/min.
2Describe the pathway of an electrical impulse through the heart, from the start of one heartbeat to ventricular contraction.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The impulse starts at the sinoatrial node in the right atrium.
  • It spreads across both atria, causing atrial systole.
  • It reaches the atrioventricular node, which delays it.
  • It passes down the bundle of His and the Purkyne tissue, causing ventricular systole from the base upwards.
3Explain how Starling’s law explains the increase in stroke volume during exercise.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Exercise increases venous return, so the ventricle fills with more blood.
  • The greater stretch of the myocardium produces a stronger contraction.
  • Therefore stroke volume increases.
4Explain how blood flow is redistributed during exercise.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The sympathetic nervous system causes vasoconstriction in arterioles supplying inactive tissue, such as the gut.
  • Vasodilation occurs in the arterioles supplying working muscles.
  • Precapillary sphincters open in the active muscles to increase blood flow.
  • This increases blood supply to the muscles that need oxygen.
5Compare the resting heart rate and stroke volume of an untrained individual and a trained athlete.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • The untrained individual has a resting heart rate of 60–80 beats per minute, compared with 30–70 for the trained athlete.
  • The trained athlete has a larger resting stroke volume, 80–120 ml, compared with 60–80 ml for the untrained individual.
  • This is because training increases the stroke volume, so the same cardiac output needs fewer beats.
