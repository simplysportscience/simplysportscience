C.4.2 Theme C · Stress & Coping SL & HL

Coping

How athletes manage stressors and their emotional responses, which types of coping suit which stressors, and how coping works before, during and after competition.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

Coping is the set of thoughts and behaviours an athlete uses to manage the demands of a stressful situation. Coping strategies are often grouped by what they target. Problem-focused coping alters or removes the stressor. Emotion-focused coping regulates the emotional response without changing the stressor, for example through relaxation, breathing or self-talk. Avoidance-focused coping moves attention or effort away from the stressor. Athletes use all three, often together. Which one works best depends on whether the stressor is controllable, and on the individual and the situation.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Define coping and distinguish problem-focused, emotion-focused and avoidance-focused coping.
  • Give examples of cognitive, somatic and social coping strategies.
  • Explain how coping strategies can be used before, during and after competition.
  • HL: Match problem-focused coping to controllable stressors and emotion-focused coping to uncontrollable stressors.
  • HL: Explain why coping effectiveness varies between individuals and situations.

Key Terms

CopingThe thoughts and behaviours used to manage the demands of a stressful situation.
StressorA demand or situation that an athlete perceives as stressful.

Deep Dive

What coping is

A stressor is a demand the athlete perceives as stressful, such as a demanding opponent, an injury, a selection decision or a crowd. Stress arises when the athlete believes the demand exceeds their resources. Coping is the response: the effort to manage the demand or the emotions it creates. Effective coping reduces the negative effects of stress on performance, well-being and health.

Three types of coping

Coping strategies are often classified by what they target:

Problem-focused, emotion-focused and avoidance-focused coping, with examples of each. The categories describe the aim of a strategy, not its quality.

Some strategies can belong to more than one category. Avoidance can be emotion-focused when it regulates anxiety, for example by telling oneself to stay calm, and seeking social support is emotion-focused when it provides reassurance and emotional regulation rather than practical help.

Cognitive, somatic and social strategies

Emotion-focused coping is often delivered through three kinds of strategy:

  • Cognitive strategies change how the athlete thinks. These include self-talk (cue words or phrases that direct attention and confidence), mental imagery (rehearsing a successful performance), and cognitive restructuring, which means identifying an unhelpful thought, such as "I always choke", and replacing it with a more balanced, useful one, such as "I've handled pressure before and I can focus on the next point".
  • Somatic strategies reduce physical arousal. These include progressive muscle relaxation (PMR), in which the athlete tenses and then relaxes muscle groups in turn, slow diaphragmatic breathing (controlled breaths that lower heart rate and tension), and biofeedback, in which sensors show heart rate or muscle tension so the athlete can learn to control them.
  • Social support comes from coaches, teammates, family and friends. It can provide reassurance, empathy and practical help. Emotional support is emotion-focused; practical advice on a tactic is problem-focused.

Routines are also important. A pre-performance routine combines cognitive and somatic elements, with a consistent sequence of breathing, cue words and imagery before each skill. It gives the athlete a familiar structure that reduces uncertainty and manages arousal.

Coping across a competition

Coping is not only needed during the event. Athletes can plan strategies for each stage: before (goal-setting, cue words and relaxation), during (refocusing, breathing and routines after mistakes) and after (reviewing performance, cooling down and seeking support). Planning across the whole competition helps athletes avoid being overwhelmed when a stressor appears.

Coping strategies across a competition. Each stage uses cognitive, somatic and social strategies, chosen to suit the stressor at that point.

Matching coping to the stressor HL

Stressors differ in whether the athlete can change them. A controllable stressor is one the athlete can influence, such as a weak technique or a training plan. The best match is usually problem-focused coping, which changes the stressor: practising the skill, seeking coaching or adjusting tactics. An uncontrollable stressor is one the athlete cannot change, such as the weather, a referee's decision or an injury that is already happening. Here emotion-focused coping is usually more effective, because the aim is to manage the emotional response and focus on what can be controlled.

Effectiveness varies HL

No coping strategy works for everyone in every situation. Effectiveness depends on the individual, including their personality, experience, preferences and skills, and on the situation, including the sport, the stage of competition and the level of pressure. Relaxation may help a nervous archer before a shot, but be unhelpful for a boxer who needs energy in the ring. Some strategies can be maladaptive: avoidance that stops an athlete facing a problem, or excessive reliance on substances to cope, can increase stress in the long term. Coping should therefore be chosen, practised in training and reviewed, rather than used at random.

Coping can also be related to injury recovery. An injured athlete may use problem-focused coping, such as following a rehabilitation programme, alongside emotion-focused coping, such as managing frustration and fear of re-injury. Both are often needed.

Exam Tips

🎯
Name the type, then the aim"Problem-focused coping aims to alter the stressor". Name the coping type, say what it targets, then give a sporting example. Check the IB command terms.
🧠
Say cognitive or somaticFor emotion-focused strategies, say whether they work on thoughts (self-talk, imagery, cognitive restructuring) or on physical arousal (breathing, PMR, biofeedback).
⚖️
Match the stressor firstFor HL questions, decide whether the stressor is controllable or uncontrollable before choosing the coping type. Then say why that match is appropriate.
🔁
Evaluate with the individual and situationSay that effectiveness depends on the athlete and the context, and give one example where a strategy would help and one where it would not.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't say that relaxation is always the best coping strategy, or that problem-focused coping always works best. The match depends on whether the stressor can be changed.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1Define coping.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • The thoughts and behaviours an athlete uses to manage the demands of a stressful situation.
2Outline the difference between problem-focused and emotion-focused coping.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Problem-focused coping alters or removes the stressor itself (for example by changing tactics or training).
  • Emotion-focused coping regulates the emotional response to the stressor without changing it (for example by breathing control or self-talk).
3Describe two somatic coping strategies used by athletes.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Progressive muscle relaxation, in which muscle groups are tensed and relaxed in turn to reduce physical tension.
  • Slow diaphragmatic breathing, which lowers heart rate and muscle tension. (Biofeedback is also accepted.)
4Explain how cognitive restructuring can help an athlete who believes they always fail under pressure.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Cognitive restructuring identifies the unhelpful belief ("I always fail under pressure") and examines the evidence for it.
  • The athlete replaces it with a more balanced, useful thought, such as recalling times they coped with pressure.
  • This reduces cognitive anxiety, which can lower the emotional response and help the athlete focus on the task.
5(HL) Explain why problem-focused coping is more appropriate for a controllable stressor, and give one example.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • A controllable stressor can be changed, so problem-focused coping, which alters or removes the stressor, addresses its cause.
  • Changing the stressor usually reduces the stress more effectively than managing the emotional response alone.
  • Example: a footballer with poor passing accuracy practises the skill with feedback from the coach, which removes the source of the stress.
  • Emotion-focused coping still helps alongside this, but is less effective on its own when the stressor can be changed.
