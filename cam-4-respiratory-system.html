The Respiratory System

The mechanics of breathing, the control of ventilation, and how oxygen and carbon dioxide are exchanged at the lungs and muscles.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions

Overview

The respiratory system brings oxygen into the body and removes carbon dioxide. During exercise, the demand for oxygen rises sharply, and breathing changes in rate and depth to meet it. This topic covers the muscles that drive breathing, the lung volumes that describe it, how breathing is controlled, and how gases are exchanged.

You need to use equations and interpret spirometer traces to calculate lung volumes and ventilation, so practise reading a trace as well as learning the definitions.

Key Terms

Tidal volumeThe volume of air breathed in or out in one normal breath.
Breathing rateThe number of breaths taken per minute.
Minute ventilationThe total volume of air moved into or out of the lungs in one minute (breathing rate × tidal volume).
Vital capacityThe maximum volume of air that can be breathed out after a maximum inhalation.
Partial pressureThe pressure exerted by one gas in a mixture, which drives diffusion from high to low.
MyoglobinA protein in muscle that stores oxygen and helps move it within the muscle cell.

Deep Dive

4.1 Mechanics and regulation of breathing

Inspiration is active. The diaphragm contracts and flattens, and the external intercostals lift the ribs, increasing the volume of the thorax. During exercise, the sternocleidomastoids, scalenes and pectoralis minor act as accessory muscles to raise the chest further.

Expiration is passive at rest, as the elastic lungs recoil. During exercise and recovery it becomes active: the internal intercostals pull the ribs down, and rectus abdominis compresses the abdomen to push the diaphragm up.

Lung volumes

Tidal volume is the volume in one normal breath. Inspiratory reserve volume is the extra air that can be inhaled after a normal breath. Expiratory reserve volume is the extra air that can be exhaled after a normal breath. Residual volume is the air remaining in the lungs after maximum exhalation.

Vital capacity is the sum of tidal, inspiratory reserve and expiratory reserve volumes. Total lung volume is vital capacity plus residual volume. Minute ventilation is breathing rate multiplied by tidal volume.

Sketch a spirometer trace, label each volume, and use the equations to calculate values from the trace. Show units (litres, and litres per minute for minute ventilation).

Regulation of breathing

The respiratory control centre in the medulla sets the rate and depth of breathing. Chemoreceptors detect changes in blood carbon dioxide and pH, which are the most important stimulus during exercise. Thermoreceptors respond to temperature, and mechanoreceptors and stretch receptors in the lungs and joints provide feedback on movement and lung inflation.

The Hering–Breuer reflex stops over-inflation of the lungs: stretch receptors signal the medulla to end inspiration. Adrenaline increases breathing rate and depth, and the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems adjust breathing during exercise and recovery.

Describe the response of each quantity in 4.1.3 before exercise, during exercise of different intensities and during recovery. Breathing rate and tidal volume both rise with intensity, which is why minute ventilation rises so steeply at maximal effort.

Long-term training

Regular training improves the breathing response: a trained athlete breathes at a lower rate at rest and during sub-maximal exercise, and achieves a larger tidal volume, so minute ventilation at maximal intensity is higher. Compare the two groups using the table in section 4.1.

4.2 Gaseous exchange and respiration

Gas exchange depends on partial pressures. In the alveoli, the partial pressure of oxygen is high and that of carbon dioxide is low, so oxygen diffuses into the blood and carbon dioxide diffuses out. Oxygen then binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells, which carry it through the blood capillaries.

At the muscles the situation is reversed. Muscle uses oxygen, so its partial pressure is low, and carbon dioxide is produced, so its partial pressure is high. Oxygen diffuses from the blood into the muscle, and carbon dioxide diffuses out. Myoglobin stores oxygen within the muscle fibre and helps move it to the mitochondria.

The diffusion gradient is what drives exchange at both sites. Anything that increases the gradient, such as more active muscle or a greater ventilation at the lungs, increases the rate of exchange.

Exam Tips

🎯
Exam tipName the accessory muscles of inspiration and say when they are used (exercise). Examiners mark the named muscles.
🧠
Exam tipFor spirometer questions, define each volume first, then calculate. Vital capacity = tidal + inspiratory reserve + expiratory reserve.
📐
Exam tipIn gas exchange, always state which way each gas diffuses and why: it always moves down its partial pressure gradient.
🔁
Exam tipMinute ventilation questions often combine two quantities. Calculate breathing rate × tidal volume and state the unit, litres per minute.

Model answer
  • Stretch receptors in the lungs detect inflation.

Model answer
  • The partial pressure of oxygen is higher in the alveoli than in the blood.

  • It then binds to haemoglobin for transport.
