Fitness Training

This unit explores the principles and methods of fitness training, and their application in designing and delivering effective fitness programmes. Students will develop practical skills in designing, implementing and evaluating fitness training programmes.

Cover this Unit
Unit 13: Fitness Training
1

Overview

Fitness training is the planned improvement of the body's ability to meet a particular demand. Good programmes are built on established principles, such as specificity, overload and progression, and they are shaped around the components of fitness that matter for the individual. This unit asks students to explain those principles, design a programme for a real person, carry it out, and judge whether it worked.

The unit is internally assessed. Students need to explain the principles and methods accurately, justify their programme design, monitor the individual during training, and review the outcome honestly.

Learning aims explained

Learning aim A: Explore the principles and methods of fitness training

Students learn the principles of training: specificity, overload, progression, reversibility, individuality, variation, recovery and adaptation. They study the health-related components of fitness (cardiovascular endurance, muscular strength and endurance, flexibility and body composition) and the skill-related components (speed, power, agility, balance, coordination and reaction time), with a test for each. They then learn the main training methods, including continuous, fartlek and interval training, HIIT and resistance training.

Strong responses Links each training method to the component of fitness it develops, and uses a test to show how that component is measured.

Learning aim B: Design a fitness training programme to meet individual needs

Students assess an individual's needs and goals, then design a programme that matches them. They select methods and set the training load, and justify each choice with reference to the principles of training, such as progressing the load gradually and building in recovery.

Strong responses Explains why each element of the programme suits this individual, rather than describing a generic plan.

Learning aim C: Implement and monitor a fitness training programme

Students carry out the programme safely and effectively, monitoring the individual's response to training. When the monitoring data or the individual's feedback shows a problem, they adapt the programme and evaluate whether the changes worked.

Strong responses Uses monitoring data, such as heart rate or perceived exertion, to decide an adaptation, and records the effect of that change.

Learning aim D: Review the effectiveness of a fitness training programme

Students review the outcomes of the programme against its goals, analyse its strengths and areas for improvement, and make justified recommendations for future improvements.

Strong responses Compares outcomes with the original goals, and recommends changes that follow directly from the evidence.

Worked examples

Sample responses show the standard expected at each level. They are examples, not model answers to copy.

PassA.P2

Describe methods of fitness training for different components of fitness.

Continuous training, such as a steady 30-minute run, develops cardiovascular endurance. Interval training, such as 30-second efforts with recovery, develops aerobic and anaerobic capacity together. Resistance training, such as a 1RM-based squat programme, develops muscular strength. Sprint interval training develops speed and power, because each sprint is short and maximal.
MeritB.M2

Justify the design of a fitness training programme with reference to the principles of training.

The programme for a 17-year-old footballer applies specificity by using sprint intervals, since the sport needs repeated speed. It applies progression by adding one set each week for three weeks, then holding the load. Recovery is built in with two rest days, which allows adaptation and reduces injury risk. Variation is used by alternating the sprint work with agility drills, which keeps the programme engaging.
DistinctionC.D2

Evaluate the effectiveness of adaptations made during implementation of the fitness training programme.

In week three, heart rate data showed the athlete was reaching maximum effort in the first set, so the recovery between sprints was extended from 30 to 60 seconds. The result was a steadier sprint time across sets, which shows the adaptation worked. However, the rest change also lowered the training stress, so the next phase needs to increase load in another way, such as a reduction in rest time from a later point in the programme.

Teaching and assessment tips

Tips
  • Name the principle before you apply it. "Progression" needs a specific example of how the load increases.
  • Every method should be matched to a component of fitness. If a method cannot be linked to a component, it should not be in the programme.
  • Use monitoring data to justify each adaptation. A change made without data is harder to defend.
  • For review, compare results with the original goals, not with general fitness improvements.
Common mistakes
  • Designing a programme that is generic rather than tailored to the individual.
  • Describing training methods without linking them to the components of fitness they develop.
  • Making changes during the programme without recording why, so the effect cannot be evaluated.
8

Assessment approach

This unit is internally assessed. Centres either set their own assignments or use an Authorised Assignment Brief approved by Pearson. Each assignment must include at least one Distinction criterion. Where a student has not reached the expected standard, the Lead Internal Verifier may authorise one resubmission.

Full information on assessment, resubmissions and retakes is in the BTEC Teacher Hub.

Key terms

SpecificityTraining that matches the demands of the sport or fitness goal.
Progressive overloadGradually increasing the demands of training so the body continues to adapt.
Fartlek trainingContinuous training with varied pace, mixing steady running with faster efforts.
ReversibilityThe loss of training adaptations when training stops or reduces.
1RMThe maximum weight a person can lift once with correct technique.
