Sports Psychology

Why do some athletes thrive under pressure while others struggle? This unit explores the psychology behind performance: personality and motivation, stress, anxiety and arousal, how teams work, and how psychological skills are planned and judged.

0

Overview

Why do some athletes thrive under pressure while others struggle? This unit explores the psychology behind performance: personality and motivation, stress, anxiety and arousal, how teams work, and how psychological skills are planned and judged.

1

A1 Personality

Personality is a consistent pattern of thoughts, feelings and behaviour. Three approaches explain how it affects performance. Trait theory says personality is stable across situations. The situational approach says behaviour depends mainly on the environment. The interactional approach combines both: a trait such as competitiveness shows more in some situations than others. Type A performers are competitive and time-urgent, which can drive motivation but also raise stress. Type B performers are more relaxed, which can reduce stress but may limit drive in a high-pressure contest.

2

A2 Motivation

Motivation is the drive to start and keep going. Intrinsic motivation comes from enjoyment and satisfaction. Extrinsic motivation comes from rewards such as medals, praise or money. Achievement motivation depends on whether a person hopes for success or fears failure. Attribution theory explains how a performer explains a result: attributing success to effort and failure to a changeable cause helps keep motivation high. Consider how a performer's type of motivation affects their persistence in training.

3

B1 Stress

Stress is the response to demands that exceed a person's ability to cope. A performer may feel stress from a difficult opponent, a selection decision or a poor result. Stress produces physical changes, such as a faster heart rate and tense muscles, and mental changes, such as poor concentration. The key distinction is between demands and the response: the same demand can cause stress in one performer and a useful challenge in another.

4

B2 Anxiety

Anxiety is a state of worry and fear about an outcome. Cognitive anxiety is the mental side: worry, negative thoughts and self-doubt. Somatic anxiety is the physical side: sweating, a racing heart and muscle tension. Each can be measured, and each affects performance differently. Cognitive anxiety usually reduces performance, while some somatic arousal can help a performer feel ready.

5

B3 Arousal

Arousal is the level of physical and mental activation. The inverted-U hypothesis proposes that performance improves as arousal rises to an optimal level, then declines. Under-aroused performers lack focus; over-aroused performers narrow their attention and make errors. Catastrophe theory adds that, when cognitive anxiety is high, performance can fall sharply once arousal passes the optimal point. The optimal level varies: fine, precise skills usually need lower arousal than strong, simple skills.

6

B4 Team dynamics

Teams develop over time. Tuckman's stages describe forming, storming, norming and performing. Task cohesion is the shared commitment to a common goal; social cohesion is the friendship and trust between members. Leadership styles shape the team, from directive to participative. Two problems often appear in groups: the Ringelmann effect, where individual effort falls as group size grows, and social loafing, where people contribute less when their individual effort is hard to see.

7

C1 Assessment

Before planning a training programme, assess the performer. Performance profiling rates a performer's psychological strengths and weaknesses. Questionnaires can measure anxiety, motivation or self-confidence. A good assessment explains what was measured, why, and what it means for the performer, so that the programme targets real needs.

8

C2 Planning

A psychological skills programme usually lasts four to six weeks. Set SMART aims: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound. Plan the sequence of sessions so that each technique builds on the last, and schedule how and when the performer will practise.

9

C3 Psychological skills and techniques

The main techniques are goal setting (process, performance and outcome goals), arousal regulation (breathing and relaxation to lower arousal, or activation to raise it), imagery (mental rehearsal of a skill or situation), confidence building (positive self-talk and reviewing successes) and concentration training (focusing on a cue and recovering focus after a distraction). Choose each technique because the performer needs it.

10

D1 Overview of psychological techniques

Each technique aims at a psychological need. Goal setting directs effort, arousal regulation manages anxiety and stress, imagery rehearses skills and builds confidence, and concentration training keeps attention on what matters. A programme works best when its techniques support one another.

11

D2 Evaluating effectiveness

Judge a programme using several types of evidence: performance results, changes in psychological measures, the performer's own report, and observation of behaviour in training and competition. One result is rarely enough. Note whether the effect lasted, and whether it applies to every performer in the same way.

12

D3 Modern influences

Modern performers are shaped by social media, pressure from sponsors and the media, and sports science tools such as performance data and wearables. These can support psychological skills, for example by showing progress, but they can also add pressure and self-comparison. Consider both sides when you evaluate.

13

D4 Making recommendations

Recommendations should follow from the evidence. A good recommendation names the change, explains why the evidence supports it, says how it would be measured and when it will be reviewed.

Practice

3 marksPractice 1

Explain personality and its effect on sports performance.

Your answer should include:
  • Define personality as a consistent pattern of thoughts, feelings and behaviour.
  • Explain trait, situational or interactional views, using one of them clearly.
  • Link a personality type to a named effect on performance, such as stress or drive.
6 marksPractice 2

Analyse how stress, anxiety and arousal theories affect performance.

Your answer should include:
  • Explain the inverted-U hypothesis and the effect of under- and over-arousal.
  • Explain the difference between cognitive and somatic anxiety.
  • Explain catastrophe theory and why the performance can fall sharply.
  • Make a judgement about which measure a coach should monitor and why.

Key terms

PersonalityA consistent pattern of thoughts, feelings and behaviours that influences performance.
Inverted-U hypothesisThe idea that performance improves with arousal up to an optimal level, then declines.
Social loafingReduced individual effort in a group, where each person's contribution is hard to see.
Performance profilingA structured assessment of a performer's psychological strengths and weaknesses.
