B.1.4 Theme B · Generating Movement in the Body SL & HL

Levers in Movement & Sport

How bones, joints and muscles act as levers, why the body trades force for speed and range of movement, and how sports equipment extends the body’s own levers.

1Overview 2Presentation 3Deep Dive 4Exam Tips 5Video 6Practice Questions
1

Overview

A lever is a rigid bar that turns around a fixed point (the fulcrum) when a force (the effort) is applied to move a load. In the body, bones act as levers, joints as fulcrums and muscles provide the effort. The class of a lever depends on the order of the fulcrum, effort and load. The lengths of the effort arm and load arm decide whether the lever gives a mechanical advantage (less force needed) or a mechanical disadvantage (more force needed, but more speed and range of movement). Most levers in the body are third class, which is why human limbs are good at fast, wide movements. Sports equipment such as a bat or racket extends the body's own levers.

By the end of this topic you should be able to

  • Identify the fulcrum, effort and load in a lever, inside and outside the body.
  • Classify a lever as first, second or third class.
  • Explain mechanical advantage and disadvantage using the effort arm and load arm.
  • Calculate the effort force needed and the mechanical advantage using torque.
  • Explain why most body levers are third class and how lever length affects speed.

Key Terms

FulcrumThe fixed point (pivot) about which a lever turns. In the body, a joint.
EffortThe force applied to move the load. In the body, usually muscle force.
LoadThe resistance being moved, such as body weight or a weight held in the hand.
Torque (moment of force)The turning effect of a force: force × perpendicular distance from the fulcrum.
Mechanical advantageEffort arm ÷ load arm. Above 1, less effort force is needed than the load.
Lever armThe perpendicular distance from the fulcrum to the line of action of a force.

Deep Dive

Levers in the body

Every lever has three parts: a fulcrum (the pivot), an effort (the force that turns the lever) and a load (the resistance). In the body, a bone is the rigid bar, a joint is the fulcrum, a muscle (pulling through its tendon) provides the effort, and the load is the weight of the body part plus anything it carries or pushes against.

The distance from the fulcrum to the effort is the effort arm, and the distance from the fulcrum to the load is the load arm. Lever class is decided by the order of the three parts:

The three classes of lever. The class tells you the order of fulcrum, effort and load. Whether there is a mechanical advantage depends on the lengths of the effort and load arms.
ClassOrderBody example (fulcrum, effort, load)Typical effect
FirstEffort – Fulcrum – LoadNodding the head back: the joint between the skull and spine, the neck extensor muscles, and the weight of the headCan change the direction of force; advantage or disadvantage depends on the arm lengths
SecondFulcrum – Load – EffortRising onto the toes: the ball of the foot, the calf muscles pulling through the Achilles tendon, and body weightAlways a mechanical advantage (the effort arm is longer), but slow and with a small range
ThirdFulcrum – Effort – LoadBiceps curl: the elbow joint, the biceps pulling near the elbow, and the weight in the handMechanical disadvantage, but speed and a large range at the end of the limb

Most levers in the body are third class, because muscles usually attach close to the joint they move. A first-class lever can have either an advantage or a disadvantage, depending on which arm is longer.

Torque and mechanical advantage

Torque (the moment of force) is the turning effect of a force about a fulcrum:

Torque = force × perpendicular distance from the fulcrum (N m)

When a lever is balanced, the turning effect of the effort equals that of the load:

Effort × effort arm = Load × load arm

Mechanical advantage (MA) compares the arms: MA = effort arm ÷ load arm. If MA is more than 1, a small effort can move a bigger load (a mechanical advantage). If MA is less than 1, the muscle must produce more force than the load (a mechanical disadvantage).

Two worked examples with illustrative values. In a biceps curl the biceps must produce seven times the load, whereas in a calf raise the effort is smaller than the load.

Why most body levers sacrifice force for speed and range

A third-class lever has a mechanical disadvantage, so the muscle needs a lot of force. In return, a small shortening of the muscle swings the end of the limb through a large distance, so the hand or foot moves quickly and through a large range. This suits sport, where speed, reach and variety of movement matter, such as kicking, throwing, sprinting and swimming. Second-class levers give a force advantage but a small range and slow movement, so they are more suited to heavy, slow tasks such as pushing off the ground.

Because a muscle's line of pull changes as the joint angle changes, the effective effort arm also changes during a movement. The muscle gets better leverage at some angles than others, which is why an exercise may feel hardest at one point in the range.

Levers outside the body: sports equipment

Bats, rackets, golf clubs and hockey sticks extend the body's own levers. A longer implement moves its tip faster for the same turning speed of the arms, which gives more speed at impact. However, the longer lever places the weight of the implement and the ball further from the fulcrum, so more torque is needed to swing it. Athletes and coaches choose the length and weight of equipment to balance the two.

For the same turning speed, the end of a longer lever travels farther in the same time and so moves faster (v = ω × r). The cost is a greater torque requirement.

The same idea explains why a golfer hits a driver further than a short iron, and why a bowler or thrower using a straight arm can release the ball faster than with a bent arm.

Exam Tips

🎯
Label the three parts firstFind the fulcrum, then the effort, then the load, then read off the order. Classify the lever only after that.
🧮
Show calculation steps and unitsWrite Effort × effort arm = Load × load arm, substitute, then give the answer in newtons (N). Keep distances in metres.
⚖️
Always state the trade-offFor third-class levers, say "mechanical disadvantage (more force needed) but greater speed and range of movement". Check the IB command terms.
🏏
Use a sporting exampleName the joint and muscle (biceps curl at the elbow) or the equipment (a hockey stick) so that your answer is applied, not just a definition.
⚠️
Common mistakeDon't call third-class levers "inefficient" and stop there. They give up force but gain speed and range of movement. Also, class alone doesn't give mechanical advantage for a first-class lever: that depends on the lengths of the arms.

Practice Questions

Write your answer first, then open the model answer to check it.

1Define torque.1 markAnswer
Model answer
  • The turning effect of a force about a fulcrum (axis), equal to the force multiplied by its perpendicular distance from the fulcrum.
2Outline why most levers in the human body are third class.2 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Muscles attach close to the joint (fulcrum), so the effort arm is short and the effort is between the fulcrum and the load.
  • This gives a mechanical disadvantage but allows the end of the limb to move quickly and through a large range of movement.
3Identify the class of lever, and the fulcrum, effort and load, in a biceps curl.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • It is a third-class lever (the effort is between the fulcrum and the load).
  • The fulcrum is the elbow joint.
  • The effort is the force of the biceps, and the load is the weight of the forearm and the object in the hand.
4A weight of 60 N acts at 0.30 m from the elbow. The biceps attaches 0.04 m from the elbow. Calculate the force the biceps must produce, and the mechanical advantage.3 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • Effort × effort arm = load × load arm, so E × 0.04 = 60 × 0.30.
  • E = 18 ÷ 0.04 = 450 N.
  • Mechanical advantage = 0.04 ÷ 0.30 = 0.13, which is a mechanical disadvantage (less than 1).
5Explain how using a longer implement, such as a golf club, affects the speed of the club head at impact and the effort required to swing it.4 marksAnswer
Model answer
  • A longer implement is a longer lever, so for the same angular velocity the tip moves further in the same time.
  • Because tip speed = angular velocity × length, the club head moves faster, so there is more speed at impact.
  • The weight of the club acts further from the fulcrum (the body), so the torque needed to swing it is greater.
  • More muscular effort is needed, so there is a trade-off between speed and the force the performer can produce.
